Cold-Ass Buttermilk, 4 AM

August 6, 2026

One of my first jobs was working the breakfast and lunch shift at a Burger King in Mobile, AL. Lunch had me on the fryers: french fries, chicken tenders, onion rings. But breakfast was my zone. I made everything on the menu, from crossan'wiches to cini-minis to biscuit sandwiches. Biscuits. That's where I'll go now.

It was my first true baking endeavor. Clock in at 4 am. Pantry first, for bags of flour, tins of baking powder, blocks of shortening. Set it all down at my rolling station, then back to the cooler for a few half-gallon cartons of buttermilk. This was the real deal. Dumping the flour on the table, measuring out the baking powder, swirling it together with my fingers, okay, that was one thing. Then I cut in the shortening, squeezing the blocks until they broke down into pebbles clinging to the flour. Then the bigger, crazier, je ne sais quoi thing: making a well in the middle and pouring in loads of cold-ass buttermilk, working it in with my whole hands, kneading it into cohesion. Man, that was it. (Not for everyone, sure. The assistant manager didn't approve, and had the crew use frozen biscuits whenever the manager was off. Duh.)

Once the dough came together I turned it out onto the floured table and did the trick that's been passed down through generations of bakers, BK or not: patted it down, folded it over on itself, patted it down again. Three, four times, no more. I'd heard somewhere that overworking it made the biscuits tough, and even at this early juncture of my baking career, I had strong opinions about lamination. "Tough crap frozen biscuits" was the phrase I used, but that's kind of what lamination is, right? Then I pulled the cutter down off the utensil shelf. BK gave us a metal ring about the diameter of a Colt-45 tall boy. Again, terminology I could relate to. I'd punch it straight down without twisting, because twisting seals the edges, and sealed edges mean the biscuits won't rise right. I figured that one out somewhere in the hum of the fryer fans. Maybe Burger King itself was zen.

I'd lay the ready-to-bake biscuits out on sheet pans, eight rows of five. I can't remember how many I made a day, but it was a lot, feels like a thousand in memory, though the real number was probably closer to eight pans, three hundred and some biscuits. If you're going through all that trouble, you might as well bake to order, in a way: forty at a time, staggered through the shift. Nothing like a fresh out of the oven biscuit. After the pan came out of the industrial oven, I'd have a tray of golden, beautiful biscuits, tops blistered and cracked. I'd butter them right on the pan and watch it disappear into all those little pockets. Even more beautiful then.

I liked doing this more than any other station. There was an order to it, different from the BK assembly line already in place around me. This was special. Flour, fat, liquid, heat: a sequence you could trust to behave itself, even at 4 am, even under fluorescent lights, even getting paid Burger King wages to do it. I didn't have the language yet for why that mattered to me. I still don't. But it's the same reason I'm still baking today. Zen.
