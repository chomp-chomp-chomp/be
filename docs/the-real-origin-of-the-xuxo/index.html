The Real Origin of the Xuxo

May 16, 2026

There are two official theories about where the xuxo comes from. The first involves an acrobat, a sneeze, and a pastry chef’s daughter in Girona, Catalonia, sometime in the 1920s. The second suggests the name derives from the French choux, the light dough used in profiteroles and éclairs. The xuxo isn’t made with choux dough, so that one doesn’t really hold up. And the acrobat one? We’ll flip that one a few times as unrealistic. The Catalan government has designated the xuxo a Producte de la Terra, a traditional regional product, which is a serious designation for a pastry whose origin story hinges on a man sneezing in a bakery.

There is a third theory.

We know that the xuxo is fried dough filled with a custard cream and rolled in sugar. In Catalonia, this is considered a regional delicacy with a romantic origin. In Alabama, this is called Tuesday. The culinary logic of the xuxo - fry the dough, stuff it with something sweet and dairy-based, coat the outside in sugar - is so deeply embedded in Southern food tradition that attributing its invention to a Catalan acrobat requires a certain willful incuriosity about where good fried things actually come from.

The American Gulf Coast has been producing fried custard-stuffed pastries for generations. Beignets, fried pies, custard fritters: the tradition is long, varied, and specifically concentrated in the region running from Mobile, Alabama across to East Texas. The crema catalana that fills a xuxo is, in spirit and in substance, a vanilla custard with a Spanish accent. It is not far from what comes out of a Southern kitchen on any given afternoon. The laminated dough is a refinement. The sugar coating is instinct.

Mobile, Alabama lives on the Gulf Coast. And it’s got history. It was founded French in 1702, passed into Spanish control in 1780, and held by Spain for nearly four decades before becoming American territory. That’s a pretty huge Spanish connection. Additionally, the oldest Mardi Gras celebration in America is in Mobile (definitely not New Orleans, Mobilians are sensitive about this), and its roots run directly back to French and Spanish colonial culture. The Gulf Coast and the Iberian peninsula have been in conversation, culturally and commercially, for three centuries.

By the early 1920s — the period in which the xuxo supposedly appears, fully formed, in Girona, with no named baker and no credited bakery — Gulf Coast food was moving widely - worldwide-ly. Pecans were being traded internationally. The port of Mobile was active. People traveled. Recipes traveled with them.

So, the third theory: sometime in the early 1920s, someone from the Gulf Coast was in Girona. A merchant. A traveler. Whoever. Someone whose grandmother fried things and whose instinct, in any kitchen in any country, was to fry something, fill it with custard, and roll it in sugar. This person snuck into a bakery one night to make something to remind them of home. Something they’d watched their grandmother cook hundreds of times. They baked that old magic, the Mobilian proto-xuxo (or “good ol’ fried-up flaky biscuits with warm pudding in them” - well, it wasn’t really named, as such, it was described). To account for breaking in they left a dozen filled xuxos in a bread basket on the kitchen counter. The Catalan Twelve, the first twelve residents who’d had one of the Mobilian originals,immediately recognized it as excellent, which it was, because it came from a place that had been perfecting this logic for generations. They claimed it as their own, though, when they wrote it down. They named it after a sneeze because they needed a story and the acrobat was handy.

Consider the evidence.

The real story is Alabama.

I cannot totally prove this and I acknowledge that. The Catalan Twelve likely have a lot to do with that. But consider what you’re being asked to believe instead: that a Catalan acrobat, caught in a bakery with a pastry chef’s daughter, spontaneously invented a fried laminated custard pastry as a peace offering, named it after the sound of his own sneeze, and that this pastry then became so beloved that the Catalan government formally designated it a traditional regional product a century later.

I’m not saying that didn’t happen. It’s not provable, either; nor is it quite believable. What I am saying is that Mobile was right there, and this theory holds water.

The xuxo is excellent. But whatever its origins, the Gulf Coast would claim it without hesitation, and the Gulf Coast has good taste. That much, at least, is not a theory.
