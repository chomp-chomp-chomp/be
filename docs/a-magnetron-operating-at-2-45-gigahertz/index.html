A Magnetron Operating at 2.45 gigahertz Was Always Going to End Here

June 13, 2026

At the center of a microwave oven is a device called a magnetron — a vacuum tube that generates microwave radiation by using a magnetic field to spin electrons in a circular path around a central cathode. As the electrons spiral outward toward a series of resonant cavities cut into a surrounding copper anode, they induce oscillating electromagnetic fields that produce microwave energy at a specific frequency. The magnetron was developed for radar during the Second World War. It was the component that made radar small enough and powerful enough to be practical in the field. It had been winning wars for several years before anyone thought to put a mug of batter in front of one.

In 1945, a Raytheon engineer named Percy Spencer was standing near an active radar magnetron in a laboratory in Massachusetts when he noticed the candy bar in his pocket had melted. He was not thinking about cake, or about food at all — he was thinking about magnetrons, which was his job. But he was curious enough to investigate what had happened, and curious enough after that to bring popcorn kernels into the lab the next day, and an egg the day after that. The egg exploded. The popcorn did not. Raytheon filed a patent before the year was out.

Two years later the company introduced the Radarange — six feet tall, seven hundred and fifty pounds, priced at around five thousand dollars, operating at 2.45 gigahertz, a frequency particularly effective at exciting water molecules, causing them to rotate rapidly and generate friction and, from that friction, heat. Rather than warming food from the outside in through the slow patient transfer of thermal energy, it excited the water molecules within the food directly, cooking from the center outward. It was marketed to restaurants and institutional kitchens, not to the emerging post-war American middle class being sold washing machines, refrigerators, and electric ranges. The domestic microwave was still twenty years away. The mug cake was closer than anyone knew.

In 1950, Rohm & Haas chemist Sandra Thompson was working at the research facilities associated with Redstone Arsenal in Huntsville, Alabama — a city that was at that particular moment becoming the unlikely center of American rocket science, a place where the air carried the smell of ambition and solid propellant and the engineering culture ran so deep it had colonized the church suppers and the office potlucks alike. Thompson had been studying how complex chemical mixtures behaved under unusual thermal conditions. It was a line of inquiry that was foundational and methodologically rigorous, but it was getting boring (and it was not obviously pointed toward anything edible). But then a joint defense contract between Rohm & Haas and Raytheon, including unfettered use of the Radarange, shifted the team's focus to rapid heating and moisture retention in military field rations. The lab was in flux.

Soon it became clear that just like the previous research, the new work was practical, unglamorous and, on its surface, shouldn't have anything to do with dessert. Flour, sugar, leavening agent, egg solids, and a lipid emulsifier should though.

A sample of the team's newest test formulation, a Theobroma Cacao Alkaloid Matrix made of the above components, found its way into a ceramic coffee mug and from there into the Radarange's test chamber. Some say Thompson ran the mug on purpose. Some people always want to blame the intern. No matter, the anticipated outcomes were thermal degradation, structural collapse, or simple inedibility — results the team had encountered time and again. What emerged this time, though, approximately seventy-five seconds later, was cake. Small, dense, self-contained, produced entirely within the vessel in which it had been made.

By all accounts the cake was genuinely good. But this means the assembled personnel had to try it. Someone proactively had to stick a spoon in the mug to see what was inside, put it in their mouth, and eat it. And then another. And another. This is worth pausing on. These were defense contractor researchers in 1950, working with prototype electromagnetic heating equipment whose biological effects on human tissue were not yet fully characterized, and when their experimental field ration batter came out of the machine as something resembling cake, they ate it. Immediately. Without apparent hesitation. Two of them, by Thompson's own account in the subsequent report, requested additional vessels. One requested that the report be expedited. The confectionery implications had not occurred to Thompson before the experiment. They occurred to her colleagues approximately ninety seconds after it concluded.

Thompson documented everything with the thoroughness it deserved. Twenty-three controlled trials. Five stable formulations. The findings appeared first as an internal Rohm & Haas / Raytheon technical report in 1952, and later in a revised form in the Journal of Food Science — accurate in every particular and entirely devoid of the wonder the subject warranted. The work introduced the theoretical framework of Rapid Internal Leavening by Dielectric Excitation, or RILDE, and proposed that the ceramic vessel was not incidental to the result but essential to it, contributing to moisture retention and thermal distribution in ways that distinguished the product categorically from anything a conventional oven could produce.

The five formulations Thompson standardized became known among Huntsville researchers as the single-stage cake vehicles — a nickname that managed to honor both the city's aerospace culture and the dessert's tendency to rise dramatically before collapsing. They spread through the laboratories and church groups and engineering offices of northern Alabama through the early 1960s, riding the informal networks of a city that was simultaneously launching rockets and feeding people at covered dish suppers. By the time the consumer microwave arrived in American kitchens in the late 1960s, the knowledge of what a mug and two minutes could produce was already out in the world, waiting.

The magnetron did not know what it was starting. Percy Spencer was thinking about radar. Sandra Thompson was thinking about field rations. Nobody was thinking about the Tuesday night at eleven p.m. when you would want cake and have neither the time nor the inclination to make one, and the answer would be sitting right there in your cabinet, two minutes away, in a mug.

Cake finds a way. It always has.
