## Introduction
<div class="em-rule">— — — — — — — — — — — —</div>

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/seal.JPG"><figcaption>Emblem sometimes associated with the Twelve. Origin and age unverified; possibly an inside joke.</figcaption></figure>

No one has ever produced a founding charter for the Catalan Twelve, which is exactly the kind of evidence they would destroy first. What survives is circumstantial: a monk here, a noblewoman there, a legend that arrives in a town fully formed with no visible author and impeccable technique. Historians call this folklore. We call it a pattern of behavior.

The working theory, which this volume does not so much argue as simply proceeds from, is that twelve individuals of unrecorded origin and excellent taste have spent several centuries moving through the world's kitchens, perfecting a small number of ideas and distributing them under whatever name the local audience would accept. A custard-filled pastry [becomes a xuxo in Girona](https://ps.chom.ps/xuxo-lore/) and something else with the pastry crimped differently forty miles down the coast. A pudding shows up in a Lancashire hotel with no prior history and a story about a chef "who just knew." This is not coincidence, or it might be, but it is at minimum a very consistent kind of coincidence. This is twelve people with a lot of time and no interest in credit.

One name keeps surfacing in the margins where the others don't: Hildevart Crumb, who elsewhere in this author's work has been documented selling the same ambiguous pastry under a [different name](https://ps.chom.ps/on-the-divergent-names/) in every port he passed through. I'd resisted connecting him to the Twelve for a while, on the grounds that one [trickster baker](https://ps.chom.ps/chomp-chomp-s-compendium/) is a bit and thirteen tricksters is a conspiracy. The ledger reprinted later in this book made that resistance harder to hold. Maybe he was one of them. Maybe he was the reason the record is this bad in the first place.

This book collects twelve recipes, each with whatever the Society left behind, real or invented, credible or not, in the margins where they were found. Some entries carry only a line or two, a scribbled aside about a dough that "already knew what it wanted to be." Others carry more, and where the record runs long enough, it gets its own space between chapters rather than crowding the recipe it's attached to. A bibliography follows at the end, for what that's worth given the subject. None of it is verified. All of it is included anyway, on the theory that a cookbook's job is to tell you what to do with your hands, and a good story is only obligated to be interesting, not true.

Whether the Twelve exist is a separate question from whether these recipes work. They do. Start with the xuxo. Everything else in this book is downstream of it, one way or another.

## I. Xuxos
<div class="em-rule">— — — — — — — — — — — —</div>

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/xuxo-hero.PNG"><figcaption>Xuxos</figcaption></figure>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending.png" class="img-light" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">
<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending-dark.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"There is no secret. The secret is waiting."
<cite>— Society's note, recovered from the endpaper of a Girona bakery ledger, translation uncertain</cite></div>

A xuxo (pronounced roughly SHOO-sho) is a yeasted, deep-fried pastry filled with vanilla pastry cream and rolled in sugar while still warm, associated with Girona since at least the early twentieth century, disputed everywhere else since approximately five minutes after that. The dough is enriched but not laminated, closer to a brioche than a croissant, which is what makes it fry properly instead of just absorbing oil and giving up.

**Yield:** 12 to 13 xuxos
**Prep:** 45 min • **Cook:** 20 min • **Inactive:** 3 hr 25 min • **Total:** 4 hr 30 min

**For the pastry cream:**
- 2 cups (480ml) whole milk
- 1 vanilla bean, split and scraped (or 2 teaspoons vanilla extract)
- 4 large egg yolks
- 1/2 cup (100g) granulated sugar
- 1/4 cup (30g) cornstarch
- pinch of salt
- 2 tablespoons (28g) unsalted butter

**For the dough:**
- 3 1/2 cups (440g) bread flour
- 1/4 cup (50g) granulated sugar
- 2 1/4 teaspoons instant yeast (one packet)
- 1 1/4 teaspoons salt
- zest of 1 lemon
- 2 large eggs
- 3/4 cup (180ml) whole milk, warmed to about 110°F (43°C)
- 6 tablespoons (85g) unsalted butter, softened

**For frying and finishing:**
- neutral oil, for deep frying (about 2 inches deep)
- 1 cup (200g) granulated sugar, for rolling

**Method:**

1. **Make the pastry cream.** Heat the milk and vanilla bean in a saucepan until just simmering. Meanwhile, whisk the egg yolks, sugar, cornstarch, and salt together until pale. Slowly pour the hot milk into the yolk mixture, whisking constantly, then return everything to the saucepan. Cook over medium heat, whisking continuously, until thickened, about 3 to 4 minutes. Remove from heat, stir in the butter, and press plastic wrap directly onto the surface. Chill at least 2 hours, or overnight.

2. **Make the dough.** In a stand mixer fitted with the dough hook, combine the flour, sugar, yeast, salt, and lemon zest. Add the eggs and warm milk, and mix on low until a shaggy dough forms. Increase to medium speed and knead for 5 minutes. With the mixer running, add the softened butter a tablespoon at a time, letting each addition incorporate before adding the next. Continue kneading until the dough is smooth, elastic, and pulls cleanly from the sides of the bowl, about 8 to 10 minutes total.

3. **First rise.** Transfer the dough to a lightly oiled bowl, cover, and let rise in a warm spot until doubled, about 1 to 1.5 hours.

4. **Shape and fill.** Turn the dough out and divide into 12 to 13 equal pieces (about 65g each). Roll each into a thin oval about 6 inches long. Place a heaping tablespoon of chilled pastry cream across the wider end, leaving a border around the edges. Fold the sides slightly inward over the filling, then roll tightly toward the pointed end, enclosing the cream completely. Pinch the seam and ends firmly closed. Place seam-side down on parchment.

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/xuxos%20-%20xuxo%20seam.PNG"><figcaption>Shape and fill</figcaption></figure>

5. **Second rise.** Cover loosely and let rise until puffy, about 45 minutes.

6. **Fry.** Heat oil to 350°F (180°C). Fry the xuxos in batches, seam-side down first, turning once, until deep golden brown all over, about 3 to 4 minutes total per batch. Drain briefly on a rack or paper towels.

7. **Finish.** While still warm, roll each xuxo in granulated sugar until fully coated. Best eaten the same day, ideally within the hour, ideally standing up.

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the filling:</strong> some versions use crema catalana (a cinnamon and citrus custard) instead of plain vanilla pastry cream. Either is defensible. Neither is verifiable as "the original."</div>

## II. Biscuits

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/biscuits.PNG"><figcaption>Biscuits</figcaption></figure>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending.png" class="img-light" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">
<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending-dark.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"Lard. Butter. Biscuit. Biscuit."
<cite>— Society's note, source disputed, possibly a forgery of a forgery</cite></div>

This is a chapter where the book has to answer for itself. A Southern buttermilk biscuit and a Catalan filled pastry have no business sharing a table of contents, except that both rely on a version of the same instinct: paying closer attention to temperature and timing than to a fixed recipe. One keeps its fat cold and its hands quick. The other proofs a soft, enriched dough on its own schedule and won't be rushed. Different mechanics, same posture toward the dough.

The biscuits presented here are Southern buttermilk drop biscuits. If you go looking for a "traditional" Southern biscuit, you'll find that everyone's grandmother makes the best biscuits and that you shouldn't talk bad about their grandma. I feel that. But all over the South, family and regional traditions included dropped, pinched, hand-shaped, and skillet-cooked biscuits, with lard, shortening, butter, or combinations thereof. This is in addition to the familiar emblematic form today, the rolled-and-cut buttermilk biscuit: the ones at fast food places, the ones on TV, the ones you dream about, even the canned pull-and-bake ones in the dairy section of the supermarket are informed by the rolled form. So what that it's the biscuit that won the battle for cultural culinary representation? In everyday cooking, someone making biscuits constantly doesn't necessarily need or want the ceremonial biscuit cutter version to keep pushing out amazing biscuits for their family and everyone else.

**Yield:** 8 to 10 biscuits
**Prep:** 15 min • **Cook:** 16 min • **Inactive:** 0 min • **Total:** 31 min

**Ingredients:**
- 2 cups (250g) all-purpose flour, plus more for dusting
- 1 tablespoon (14g) baking powder
- 1/4 teaspoon baking soda
- 1 teaspoon fine salt
- 1 tablespoon (12g) granulated sugar
- 1/2 cup (96g) vegetable shortening, preferably Crisco, chilled
- 1 cup (240ml) cold buttermilk, plus more for brushing

**Method:**

1. **Mix the dry ingredients.** Put the flour, baking powder, baking soda, salt, and sugar in a bowl and whisk to combine.

2. **Cut in the shortening.** Add the chilled shortening to the dry ingredients and work it in with your fingers or a pastry cutter until the mixture resembles coarse meal, with a few small pieces remaining. Work quickly and don't overdo it. You're looking for a tender biscuit, not a perfectly uniform mixture.

3. **Add the buttermilk.** Make a well in the center and pour in the cold buttermilk. Stir with a fork just until a thick, shaggy dough comes together, loose enough to drop from a spoon rather than roll out. Don't overmix.

4. **Preheat.** Heat the oven to 450°F (230°C). Grease a 10-inch cast iron skillet with shortening.

5. **Drop.** Using a large spoon or a 1/4-cup scoop, drop mounds of dough into the prepared skillet, spacing them so the sides touch. They'll support each other as they rise.

6. **Bake.** Brush the tops with a little buttermilk. Bake for 14 to 16 minutes, until tall and deeply golden on top.

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the fat:</strong> All should be cold. Crisco gives these a soft, short, tender crumb but is less temperamental about temperature than butter. Lard: cold. Butter can be substituted in 1:1 volume for more flavor and a somewhat flakier biscuit; lard will produce an especially tender, savory crumb. The Society's note doesn't specify. People who claim to know have enemies.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the stovetop method:</strong> These can also be cooked entirely on the stove. Grease a covered 10-inch cast iron skillet with 2 tablespoons shortening and heat over low to medium-low heat. Drop the biscuits in, cover, and cook until browned underneath and cooked most of the way through, then turn and brown the other side. This is simply how biscuits are made in some households. And for a reason. The result is softer and denser than an oven biscuit, with a crisp, skillet-browned exterior.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the pan:</strong> These can also be baked on a parchment-lined sheet instead of in the skillet, spaced an inch apart. They'll turn out taller with crisper sides all around, but you lose the soft, pull-apart sides the skillet gives you where the biscuits touch.</div>

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/biscuits%20-%20skillet.PNG"><figcaption>Biscuits</figcaption></figure>

<div class="marginalia">My grandma used to make drop biscuits, always using Crisco over lard or butter. She dolloped it out of those tubs with her hands; her palm was the unit of measurement. They were more tender and you could taste the buttermilk more, she said. That's good for you. She made them for breakfast, lunch, dinner, and supper (there are at least four types of meals in the South). She also said she liked it so she could sing Mama's little baby loves shortnin', shortnin', / Mama's little baby loves shortnin' bread.</div>

## Interlude: On the Matter of Provenance

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/Interlude%201%20-%20Manuscript.jpeg"><figcaption>Recovered ledger page</figcaption></figure>

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/Interlude%201%20-%20street%20scene.jpeg"><figcaption>Girona, or the Gulf Coast, or both</figcaption></figure>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending.png" class="img-light" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">
<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending-dark.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"Some roads only look like they're going in one direction. <s>East</s>." "No. West."
<cite>— ledger fragment, undated, two sets of handwriting, East is crossed out in first phrase</cite></div>

Here is what the ledger actually says, as best as it can be transcribed. A name, half legible. A route: Girona, then a gap of years, then a port on the Gulf Coast, then a gap of years, then Girona again. In the margin, in different ink, someone has written *the dough doesn't care what you call it*, which could be a philosophy or could just be a note to self from someone tired of arguing about names.

It would be easy to make too much of this. A biscuit is not a xuxo. One is built from cold fat cut straight into the dough and dropped into a hot skillet, the other is enriched, shaped, and fried. They don't look alike, they don't taste alike, and no one is claiming otherwise. What they share is narrower and, I think, more interesting: not a shared technique but a shared relationship to the dough, the conviction that it will tell you when it's ready if you're willing to stop manhandling it and listen, whether that means keeping the fat cold or trusting a long, slow rise instead of rushing it. That's not a recipe. That's closer to a house style, the kind that survives a change of continent because it was never written down in the first place, only shown.

Whether one person carried that house style from a Catalan kitchen to a Gulf Coast one, or the reverse, or whether it simply arose twice because cold fat behaves the same way everywhere, is not something this book is prepared to settle. I will say that the ledger's gap of years lines up, roughly, with a period during which several other entries in this book also go quiet, and that I don't love how neatly that lines up, and that I am choosing to include it anyway.

Make of the half-legible name what you will. I've looked at it more times than I'd like to admit and I still think the first letter is a C. The rest could be Homp, could be something else entirely, could be my eyes doing what tired eyes do to old ink. I'm not chasing it further.

## III. Cardamom Buns

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/cardamom.PNG"><figcaption>Cardamom Buns</figcaption></figure>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending.png" class="img-light" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">
<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending-dark.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"Told the baker it was tradition. It was Tuesday. She believed me. By Thursday it was true."
<cite>— Society's note, found in Swedish, translation approximate</cite></div>

Kardemummabullar, cardamom buns, are the Swedish sibling of the more famous cinnamon bun, built on the same enriched dough but filled with butter, sugar, and freshly ground cardamom instead. The knotted shape, rather than a simple spiral, isn't decorative. It's a structural secret, meant to leave more surface area exposed so the filling caramelizes at the edges instead of staying sealed inside. Whole cardamom pods, cracked and ground just before using, matter more here than in almost any other recipe in this book. Pre-ground cardamom loses its punch fast, and this bun has nowhere to hide a weak one.

**Yield:** 16 buns
**Prep:** 35 min • **Cook:** 10 min • **Inactive:** 1 hr 35 min • **Total:** 2 hr 20 min

**For the dough:**
- 4 cups (500g) all-purpose flour
- 1/3 cup (65g) granulated sugar
- 2 1/4 teaspoons instant yeast (one packet)
- 1 teaspoon salt
- 2 teaspoons freshly ground cardamom
- 1 1/4 cups (300ml) whole milk, warmed to about 110°F (43°C)
- 1 large egg
- 6 tablespoons (85g) unsalted butter, softened

**For the filling:**
- 8 tablespoons (113g) unsalted butter, very soft
- 1/2 cup (100g) packed brown sugar
- 2 tablespoons (12g) freshly ground cardamom
- 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon

**For finishing:**
- 1 egg, beaten, for egg wash
- pearl sugar or coarse sanding sugar

**Method:**

1. **Make the dough.** In a stand mixer fitted with the dough hook, combine the flour, sugar, yeast, salt, and cardamom. Add the warm milk and egg, and mix on low until a shaggy dough forms. Knead on medium speed for 5 minutes, then add the softened butter a tablespoon at a time, letting each addition incorporate before adding more. Knead until smooth and elastic, about 8 minutes.

2. **First rise.** Transfer to a lightly oiled bowl, cover, and let rise until doubled, about 1 hour.

3. **Fill.** Turn the dough out and roll into a rectangle roughly 20 by 12 inches. Spread the soft butter evenly over the surface, then sprinkle with the brown sugar, cardamom, and cinnamon.

4. **Fold and cut.** Fold the rectangle into thirds like a letter, then roll gently to seal and even it out. Cut into 16 strips, each about 3/4 inch wide.

5. **Knot.** Take one strip, twist it several times to expose the layered filling, then loosely knot it around two fingers and tuck the tail underneath. Place each knot on a parchment-lined baking sheet, spaced apart.

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/cardamom%20buns%20-%20knot.PNG"><figcaption>Kno</figcaption></figure>

6. **Second rise.** Cover loosely and let rise until puffy, about 30 to 40 minutes. Preheat the oven to 425°F (220°C).

7. **Bake.** Brush each bun with egg wash and sprinkle generously with pearl sugar. Bake for 8 to 10 minutes, until deeply golden. Cool slightly on a rack before eating.

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the knot:</strong> if the first few come out lopsided, that's normal, not a sign of bad technique. Even bakeries that make hundreds a day let the ugly ones sell first, on the theory that a slightly imperfect bun looks more handmade and therefore more trustworthy. Whether that's marketing or philosophy depends entirely on how the day is going.</div>

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"A knot holds more heat than a spiral. This is a secret."
<cite>— ledger fragment, undated, next to equations</cite></div>

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/cardamom%202.JPG"><figcaption>Cardamom Buns</figcaption></figure>

## IV. King Cake

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/King%20Cake.jpeg"><figcaption>King Cake</figcaption></figure>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending.png" class="img-light" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">
<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending-dark.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"I hid a porcelain baby in the bread. It chipped a tooth. Don't eat the baby."
<cite>— Society's note, on a torn-out piece of a hymnal sharing page with Clarum decus jejunii, unclear if intentionally</cite></div>

King cake sits at the overlap of Mardi Gras tradition and a much older European habit of baking something valuable into bread and letting the finder take on a role, good or bad, for the rest of the season. The New Orleans version, a ring of brioche-like dough striped in purple, green, and gold sugar with a plastic baby hidden inside, is the one most people know, and the one most people get wrong first. Mobile, Alabama held the earliest documented Mardi Gras celebrations in the country, well before New Orleans claimed the holiday as its own. This recipe gives a better cake.

**Yield:** one 12- to 14-inch ring, serves 12 to 16
**Prep:** 35 min • **Cook:** 30 min • **Inactive:** 2 hr 25 min • **Total:** 3 hr 30 min

**For the dough:**
- 4 1/2 cups (565g) all-purpose flour, plus more as needed
- 1/2 cup (100g) granulated sugar
- 2 1/4 teaspoons instant yeast (one packet)
- 1 teaspoon salt
- zest of 1 lemon
- 1 teaspoon ground nutmeg
- 3/4 cup (180ml) whole milk, warmed to about 110°F (43°C)
- 3 large eggs, room temperature
- 10 tablespoons (142g) unsalted butter, softened

**For the cinnamon filling:**
- 1/2 cup (100g) packed brown sugar
- 2 tablespoons (16g) ground cinnamon
- 4 tablespoons (57g) unsalted butter, very soft

**For the icing and finishing:**
- 2 cups (240g) powdered sugar
- 3 to 4 tablespoons (45 to 60ml) milk or lemon juice
- purple, green, and gold sanding sugar
- 1 small plastic baby or dried bean, for hiding

**Method:**

1. **Make the dough.** In a stand mixer fitted with the dough hook, combine the flour, sugar, yeast, salt, lemon zest, and nutmeg. Add the warm milk and eggs, and mix on low until a shaggy dough forms. Knead on medium speed for 5 minutes, then add the softened butter a tablespoon at a time, letting each addition incorporate before adding more. Continue kneading until smooth and elastic, about 8 to 10 minutes. The dough will be soft and slightly tacky.

2. **First rise.** Transfer to a lightly oiled bowl, cover, and let rise in a warm place until doubled, about 1.5 hours.

3. **Fill.** Turn the dough out onto a floured surface and roll into a large rectangle, roughly 24 by 12 inches. Spread the soft butter over the surface, then sprinkle evenly with the brown sugar and cinnamon.

4. **Roll and shape.** Starting from a long edge, roll the dough tightly into a log. Bring the two ends together to form a ring, pinching firmly to seal. Transfer to a parchment-lined baking sheet.

5. **Second rise.** Cover loosely and let rise until puffy, about 45 minutes. Preheat the oven to 350°F (180°C).

6. **Bake.** Bake for 25 to 30 minutes, until deeply golden and a thermometer inserted into the dough reads about 190°F (88°C). Tent with foil partway through if the top is browning too fast. Cool completely on a rack before icing.

7. **Hide the prize and finish.** If using a plastic baby, cut a small slit in the underside of the cooled cake and push the baby completely inside. If using a dried bean, it may instead be enclosed in the dough before baking. Whisk the powdered sugar with milk or lemon juice until a thick, pourable icing forms. Pour over the cooled cake, letting it drip down the sides, and immediately sprinkle bands of purple, green, and gold sugar over the wet icing before it sets.

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the baby:</strong> tradition holds that whoever finds it hosts the next gathering, or buys the next king cake, depending on which household you ask. Warn unknowing guests that one is hidden inside before serving.</div>

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/king%20cake%202.JPG"><figcaption>King Cake</figcaption></figure>

They all throw beads and doubloons and, sometimes, moon pies. Never King Cakes. Maybe throwing a whole cake to the crowd would seem impractical, but I disagree. Toss them like frisbees. The Catalan Twelve's signature throw. Premium. If you're going to do it anyway, don't flash anyone for less than a King Cake.</div>

## V. Sweet Potato Pie

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/spp-hero.PNG"><figcaption>Sweet Potato Pie</figcaption></figure>

Sweet potato pie is a real, documented staple of African American and Southern cooking, grown widely, stored well, and kept a Southern pantry fed through a season without depending on anything canned. This version comes from a real family recipe, not the book's usual apparatus of disputed provenance and half-legible ledgers. No Society note attached to this one. It didn't need one.

**Yield:** 2 deep-dish 9-inch pies
**Prep:** 40 min • **Cook:** 1 hr 15 min • **Inactive:** 1 hr 5 min • **Total:** 3 hr

**Filling:**
- 3 1/2 to 4 cups (about 525 to 600g) sweet potatoes, peeled and cut into 1-inch cubes
- 1 cup (226g) butter
- 1/2 cup (100g) white sugar
- 1 1/2 cups (300g) brown sugar
- 3 eggs, separated
- 1 1/2 teaspoons baking powder
- 1 cup (240ml) evaporated milk
- 1/4 teaspoon salt
- 1 teaspoon cinnamon
- 1/4 teaspoon ginger
- pinch cloves
- 1/8 teaspoon allspice
- 1 1/2 teaspoons vanilla extract

**Crust (for 2 single-crust pies):**
- 2 1/2 cups (315g) all-purpose flour
- 1/2 teaspoon salt
- 1 cup (226g) cold butter
- 6–10 tablespoons (90 to 150ml) ice water

**Method:**

1. **Make the crusts.** Combine the flour and salt; cut in the cold butter until crumbly. Gradually add the ice water, tossing with a fork until the dough holds together when pressed. Divide in half, wrap each half in plastic wrap, and refrigerate 1 hour. Roll out and fit into two 9-inch pie pans.

2. **Cook the potatoes.** Put the cubed potatoes in a pot and add enough water to cover. Bring to a boil. Reduce heat; cook, uncovered, until fork-tender, 13–15 minutes.

3. **Mash and mix.** Mash hot potatoes with butter. Add sugar. Beat egg yolks and add along with evaporated milk, vanilla, and spices.

4. **Let cool.** Let the mixture cool to room temperature.

5. **Fold and fill.** Stir in baking powder. Beat egg whites and fold in. Divide the filling between the two uncooked pastry shells.

6. **Bake.** Bake at 450°F (230°C) for 15 minutes. Reduce heat to 350°F (180°C) and bake about 45 minutes more.

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on brown butter:</strong> swap the 1 cup butter for browned butter. Melt it in a light-colored saucepan over medium heat, swirling occasionally, until it foams, turns golden, then smells nutty with small brown flecks settled at the bottom, about 4 to 6 minutes. Pour immediately into a heatproof bowl, scraping in the browned bits, and let cool slightly before mashing into the hot sweet potatoes as the recipe directs.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on benne (sesame) variation:</strong> toast 2 to 3 tablespoons of benne (sesame) seeds in a dry skillet over medium heat, stirring often, until fragrant and lightly golden, 2 to 3 minutes. Fold half into the pastry dough before it's rolled out, and scatter the rest over the filling before baking. Benne carries deep roots in Southern and Lowcountry cooking.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on adding bourbon:</strong> stir 1 to 2 tablespoons of bourbon into the filling along with the vanilla and spices. It's a common addition in Southern sweet potato pie elsewhere.</div>

## VI. Sticky Toffee Pudding

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"Sauce first. Not a question."
<cite>— ledger fragment, water-stained</cite></div>

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/Sticky%20Toffee%20Pudding.jpeg"><figcaption>Sticky Toffee Pudding</figcaption></figure>

Sticky toffee pudding is often credited to a specific English hotel kitchen in the mid-twentieth century, a suspiciously tidy origin story for a technique this old and this widespread. I did come across a ledger fragment scrawled sometime in the 1960s about the Society being in some unnamed hotel kitchen. Of course it was. Also tidy. And dismissed for now.

The technique is to have the dates blitzed with baking soda and boiling water to break them down and sweeten a sponge from the inside, finished under a hot toffee sauce that soaks in rather than sitting on top. Done properly, it's less a cake with sauce poured over it than a single dessert built in two stages that meet in the middle. This version goes further than the pub standard, browning the butter for the sponge and building the sauce with muscovado sugar and a touch of dark rum, on the theory that if you're already going to the trouble, you may as well go all the way.

**Yield:** 8 individual puddings, or one 9-inch pan, serves 8
**Prep:** 25 min • **Cook:** 35 min • **Inactive:** 5 min • **Total:** 1 hr 5 min

**For the sponge:**
- 8 ounces (about 1 1/3 cups) pitted Medjool dates, roughly chopped
- 1 teaspoon baking soda
- 1 cup (240ml) boiling water
- 6 tablespoons (85g) unsalted butter
- 3/4 cup (150g) packed dark brown sugar
- 2 large eggs, room temperature
- 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
- 1 1/2 cups (190g) all-purpose flour
- 1 3/4 teaspoons baking powder
- 1/4 teaspoon fine salt

**For the toffee sauce:**
- 1 cup (240ml) heavy cream
- 1/2 cup (113g) unsalted butter
- 3/4 cup (150g) dark muscovado sugar (or substitute packed dark brown sugar)
- 2 tablespoons (40g) golden syrup or dark corn syrup
- 1 tablespoon (15ml) dark rum (optional, but recommended)
- 1/2 teaspoon flaky sea salt

**Method:**

1. **Brown the butter.** Melt the 6 tablespoons of butter in a light-colored saucepan over medium heat, swirling occasionally, until it foams, then turns golden, then releases a nutty aroma and small brown flecks settle at the bottom, 4 to 6 minutes. Immediately pour into a heatproof bowl to stop the cooking, scraping in the browned bits. Let cool slightly.

2. **Macerate the dates.** Combine the chopped dates and baking soda in a heatproof bowl. Pour the boiling water over them and let sit for 10 minutes. The soda raises the pH and softens the dates well past what soaking alone would do, which is what lets them dissolve into the batter instead of sitting in it as chunks. Once softened, blend the mixture (an immersion blender works well) into a rough, thick paste. A few small pieces remaining is fine.

3. **Preheat and prep.** Preheat the oven to 350°F (180°C). Butter 8 ramekins or a 9-inch cake pan.

4. **Build the batter.** Whisk the browned butter and dark brown sugar together until combined. Whisk in the eggs one at a time, then the vanilla. Fold in the flour, baking powder, and salt just until no dry streaks remain, then fold in the date paste until evenly distributed. The batter will be loose, closer to a thick cake batter than a dough.

5. **Bake.** Divide among the ramekins or pour into the prepared pan. Bake ramekins for 18 to 20 minutes, or the full pan for 30 to 35 minutes, until a skewer inserted in the center comes out clean and the top springs back when pressed.

6. **Make the toffee sauce.** While the sponge bakes, combine the cream, butter, muscovado sugar, and golden syrup in a saucepan over medium heat. Bring to a gentle boil, whisking occasionally, and cook for 3 to 4 minutes until slightly thickened and glossy. Remove from heat and stir in the rum, if using, and the flaky salt.

7. **Finish.** While the sponge is still warm, poke a few holes in the top with a skewer and pour about a third of the toffee sauce over it, letting it soak in. Rest 5 minutes, then unmold and serve with additional warm sauce poured over the top.

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the rum:</strong> dark rum in a British pudding is a modern addition, not a historical one. Included here anyway. Because it's a good one.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on simplifying:</strong> For a quicker, more traditional version, three swaps bring this back to the standard pub-style pudding. Melt the 6 tablespoons of butter for the sponge instead of browning it. Soften the dates as directed, then mash with a fork instead of blending into a paste. For the sauce, use just 1 cup heavy cream, 1/2 cup butter, and 3/4 cup packed brown sugar, boiled together until slightly thickened, skipping the golden syrup and rum.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on substituting the dates:</strong> Pitted prunes can replace the dates 1:1 by weight. Soak and blend them the same way. The sponge will be just as dark and moist, with a deeper, slightly less caramel-like fruitiness.</div>

## VII. Gougères

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/gougeres-hero.PNG"><figcaption>Gougères</figcaption></figure>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending.png" class="img-light" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">
<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending-dark.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"Cook first. Sometimes baking is not enough."
<cite>— ledger fragment, undated, in a hand that appears nowhere else</cite></div>

A gougère is a small savory cheese puff, and it is made from the same paste as an éclair, a profiterole, a cream puff, a croquembouche, and much of the pastry counter in any French bakery. One dough. All of those names. The French did this to themselves in just over two centuries. By the 1800s they were swimming in choux just by playing with paste and making things seem hard.

It's usually attributed to a pastry chef in Catherine de' Medici's traveling household in 1540. You'll likely be much more interested in the fact that this is the only dough in the collection that gets cooked before it gets baked. Flour into boiling butter and water, stirred on the stove until it dries into a paste, then eggs beaten in one at a time until it slumps. There is no yeast in it and no baking powder. It rises on nothing but the water inside it turning to steam. And that needs to go somewhere. Everything else in this collection rises because something living or chemical was added to it. This one rises with just water and heat.

**Yield:** about 30 small gougères
**Prep:** 25 min • **Cook:** 30 min • **Inactive:** 0 min • **Total:** 55 min

**Ingredients:**
- 1/2 cup (120ml) water
- 1/2 cup (120ml) whole milk
- 8 tablespoons (113g) unsalted butter, cubed
- 1 teaspoon fine salt
- 1/2 teaspoon freshly ground black pepper
- 1 cup (125g) all-purpose flour
- 4 large eggs, room temperature, plus a fifth if needed
- 5 ounces (142g) Gruyère or sharp cheddar, grated, plus more for topping
- 1 teaspoon Dijon mustard (optional)
- pinch of freshly grated nutmeg (optional)

**Method:**

1. **Preheat and prep.** Heat the oven to 400°F (200°C). Line two baking sheets with parchment.

2. **Boil the liquid.** Combine the water, milk, butter, salt, and pepper in a medium saucepan over medium-high heat. Bring to a full rolling boil with the butter completely melted. Don't let it simmer along slowly, or you'll lose liquid to steam before the flour ever gets there.

3. **Cook the paste.** Dump in all the flour at once and stir hard with a wooden spoon until it pulls together into a single ball. Keep cooking and stirring another 2 minutes, until a thin film forms on the bottom of the pan and the paste smells faintly toasted. This step is drying the paste out. A drier paste holds more egg, more egg makes more steam, and more steam is the entire leavening.

4. **Cool it down.** Transfer to a bowl and let sit 3 to 5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until you can rest your hand against the side of the bowl. Add eggs to a hot paste and you get sweetened scrambled egg with a cheese problem.

5. **Beat in the eggs.** Add the eggs one at a time, beating each in completely before the next. The paste will look broken and wrong after every single addition and come back together every single time. After the fourth egg it should fall from the spoon in a thick ribbon that holds a V for a second before dropping. If it's still stiff, beat in part of a fifth egg until it does.

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/gougeres%20-%20choux%20batter.PNG"><figcaption>Holds for a V</figcaption></figure>

6. **Add the cheese.** Stir in the grated cheese, the Dijon, and the nutmeg, if using.

7. **Scoop.** Drop rounded tablespoons onto the sheets, spaced 2 inches apart. Smooth any peaks down with a wet fingertip; a peak will scorch before the rest is done. Scatter a little more cheese over the tops.

8. **Bake.** 20 to 25 minutes, until deeply golden brown and firm to the tap. Do not open the oven for the first 15 minutes. They are held up by steam and nothing else, and a draft at the wrong moment collapses them permanently.

9. **Dry them out.** Pierce each one with the tip of a knife, turn the oven off, and leave them inside with the door cracked for 5 minutes. This lets the trapped steam escape instead of soaking the shell from the inside on the way to the table.

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the cheese:</strong> pre-shredded bags are coated in anti-caking starch that keeps them from melting cleanly. Grating a block yourself takes an extra five minutes and produces a noticeably better melt. Don't cheat. Just grate the block.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the fifth egg:</strong> the last egg is a judgment call, not a measurement. Eggs vary, and so does how long you cooked the paste. The recipe can tell you what the paste should look like; it cannot tell you when you're there. Same as everything else in this book.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on making them ahead:</strong> the piped or scooped mounds freeze raw on the sheet, then keep in a bag for a month. Bake straight from frozen, adding 3 or 4 minutes. This is the only recipe in the collection that gets easier the second time in a way you can put in the freezer.</div>

## VIII. Baklava

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/baklavahero.PNG"><figcaption>Baklava</figcaption></figure>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending.png" class="img-light" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">
<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending-dark.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"No need to agree on a thing to enjoy it."
<cite>— ledger fragment, undated</cite></div>

Baklava's origin is one of the oldest and least settled arguments in food history, claimed at different points by Greek, Turkish, and broader Levantine kitchens, with the layered, syrup-soaked version most people recognize today usually credited to the Ottoman court kitchens that popularized it centuries later. Any way it goes, nobody with any involvement in that argument has ever fully backed down, even when they aren't taking credit. This version leans into pistachio, one of the most traditional fillings across the whole disputed family tree, and rosewater in the syrup, a pairing common enough across the region that it undercuts any single country's claim to have invented the combination outright.

One note before you start: this recipe uses store-bought phyllo. Anyone telling you to make your own at home has either never actually done it, or wants you to suffer the way they did. Professional bakeries buy it. You should too.

**Yield:** one 9x13-inch pan, about 24–32 pieces
**Prep:** 45 min • **Cook:** 50 min • **Inactive:** 4 hr • **Total:** 5 hr 35 min

**For the filling:**
- 3 cups (360g) shelled pistachios, finely chopped (not ground to dust, you want texture)
- 1/3 cup (65g) granulated sugar
- 1 teaspoon ground cinnamon
- pinch of salt

**For the pastry:**
- 1 pound (454g) store-bought phyllo dough, thawed according to package instructions
- 1 1/4 cups (2 1/2 sticks/280g) unsalted butter, melted

**For the syrup:**
- 1 cup (200g) granulated sugar
- 1 cup (240ml) water
- 1 tablespoon (15ml) lemon juice
- 1 to 2 teaspoons rosewater (start with 1, taste before adding more)
- 1 tablespoon (20g) honey (optional, for a rounder syrup)

**Method:**

1. **Make the syrup first.** Combine the sugar, water, and lemon juice in a saucepan and bring to a boil, stirring until the sugar dissolves. Simmer 5 minutes. Remove from heat, stir in the honey (if using) and rosewater. Taste it, it should smell like flowers in the background, not announce itself. Let cool completely while you build the baklava; it needs to be cold when it meets the hot pastry.

2. **Prep the filling.** Pulse the pistachios in a food processor in short bursts until finely chopped but not powder. Stir in the sugar, cinnamon, and salt.

3. **Preheat and prep the pan.** Preheat the oven to 350°F (180°C). Butter a 9x13-inch baking pan.

4. **Layer the base.** Keep the phyllo covered with a damp towel while you work, it dries out and cracks fast, and dry phyllo isn't coming back. Lay one sheet in the pan, brush thoroughly with melted butter all the way to the edges. Repeat with 8 more sheets, buttering each one.

5. **Add the filling.** Spread about a third of the pistachio mixture evenly over the layered phyllo.

6. **Repeat the layers.** 5 more buttered sheets, another third of the filling, 5 more buttered sheets, the last third of the filling, then a final stack of 8 buttered sheets on top.

7. **Cut before baking.** Using a sharp knife, cut all the way through to the bottom of the pan, into diamonds or squares. Much easier now than after baking.

8. **Bake.** Bake at 350°F (180°C) for 45 to 50 minutes, until deep golden brown across the whole top, not just the edges.

9. **Soak.** As soon as it comes out of the oven, pour the cooled syrup evenly over the top, right along the cuts. Let sit uncovered at room temperature at least 4 hours, ideally overnight, before serving.

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the phyllo:</strong> it will try to dry out and crack the entire time you're working with it. Keep the stack you're not actively using under a slightly damp towel, and move quickly once you start. A few torn sheets won't show in the finished pan, nobody sees the second layer down. Don't patch, just keep going.</div>

## IX. Coca de Recapte

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/coca-hero.PNG"><figcaption>Coca de Recapte</figcaption></figure>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending.png" class="img-light" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">
<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending-dark.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"They put oil in the diners' hands before my wine even arrived."
<cite>— ledger ephemera, on the back of a wine list, ink smudged, probably a sommelier</cite></div>

Coca is the flatbread Catalonia doesn't bother arguing about with anyone else, mostly because everyone else's flatbread already gets called something else. Focaccia gets its dimples, pissaladière gets its anchovies, pizza gets the world, and coca just sits there being older than most of the argument, thin, oval, entirely unbothered - completely delicious.

The version here is coca de recapte, built on escalivada, meaning the vegetables get roasted whole until their skins blacken and give up, then peeled and dressed rather than diced and cooked down. Recapte, roughly, means "the goods on hand," which is either a name for a technique or an instruction to stop shopping and start using what's already in the kitchen. Both are true.

The name outlasted the food it once described. Coca traces back through Old Dutch to the same root as the German kuchen and the English cake, a lineage that has nothing to do with flatbread and everything to do with how far a word can wander from the thing it started attached to. Nobody had to hide anything for that to happen. Language did it on its own, which may be the more unsettling version of the same trick.

**Yield:** two 10x14-inch cocas, serves 6 to 8
**Prep:** 45 min • **Cook:** 1 hr 15 min • **Inactive:** 1 hr 15 min • **Total:** 3 hr 15 min

**For the escalivada:**
- 2 medium eggplants
- 2 red bell peppers
- 1 large yellow onion, unpeeled, halved
- 3 tablespoons (45ml) olive oil, plus more for finishing
- salt

**For the dough:**
- 3 1/2 cups (440g) bread flour
- 1 1/2 teaspoons instant yeast
- 1 1/2 teaspoons fine salt
- 1 1/4 cups (300ml) warm water
- 3 tablespoons (45ml) olive oil

**To finish:**
- flaky salt
- optional: 4 ounces (113g) cured anchovy fillets or thinly sliced botifarra

**Method:**

1. **Char the vegetables.** Heat the oven to 450°F (230°C). Rub the eggplants, peppers, and onion halves with a little of the olive oil and roast directly on a sheet pan, turning occasionally, until the skins are fully blackened and blistered and the flesh has collapsed, 35 to 45 minutes for the peppers and onion, up to an hour for the eggplant. Transfer to a bowl, cover with plastic wrap, and let steam for 15 minutes, this loosens the skins far more than patience alone would.

2. **Peel and dress.** Once cool enough to handle, peel the blackened skins away, discard the seeds and stems, and tear the flesh into long strips by hand, never a knife. Toss with the remaining olive oil and a pinch of salt. Don't rinse anything to remove stray char; that's flavor, not a mistake.

3. **Make the dough.** Whisk the flour, yeast, and salt together. Add the warm water and olive oil, and stir until a shaggy dough forms. Knead by hand or in a stand mixer until smooth and elastic, about 8 minutes. It should feel leaner and less tacky than an enriched dough, this one isn't trying to be bread.

4. **Rise.** Cover and let rise until doubled, about 1 hour.

5. **Shape.** Divide the dough in two. On a floured surface, roll or stretch each piece into a thin oval, about 1/4 inch thick, and transfer to parchment-lined sheet pans.

6. **Top.** Lay the escalivada strips over the dough in loose, uneven ribbons, leaving a bit of dough exposed at the edges. Scatter the anchovies or botifarra over top, if using.

7. **Bake.** Heat the oven to 475°F (245°C). Bake 12 to 15 minutes, until the exposed dough is deeply golden and the edges are crisp.

8. **Finish.** Drizzle with more olive oil and a scatter of flaky salt straight out of the oven. Best warm, cut into strips rather than squares.

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the char:</strong> don't rush this step by roasting at a lower, gentler heat. The vegetables need to blacken fully, not just soften, or the escalivada turns watery instead of smoky. A little burnt is correct here in a way it almost never is anywhere else in this book.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the dough:</strong> coca dough is meant to be thinner and crisper underfoot than focaccia's. If it springs back stubbornly while you're shaping it, let it rest 5 minutes and try again rather than forcing it.</div>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending-dark.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"Sometimes cakes forget to be sweet."
<cite>— ledger fragment, undated</cite></div>

<div class="marginalia">While researching this book, I went down lots of rabbit holes. Some of them led me through history, some through language, and some back to Catalonia. You've seen that some of the Society fragments are possible mistranslations. I found out that I had the idea and translation of Coca wrong, or partially wrong, all along. I'm a fan of diet soda, especially Diet Coke. Coca. But my Coca is a Spanish word borrowed from the Quechua language of the Inca Empire and references the indigenous South American plant. I saw Coca in a Catalan baking book and said, "Oh man! Finally a pastry to complement my Diet Coke!" Like maybe a sugar syrup infused with coca leaves in a cake. No, I learned this Coca is a type of flatbread, typically with savory toppings, and its etymology is borrowed from the Old Dutch coca - and shares Proto-Germanic lineage with modern German kuchen and English cake. Still cake, different kind. Still compatible with cola.</div>

## X. Pastéis de Nata


<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/Pastel%20de%20Nata.jpeg"><figcaption>Pastéis de Nata</figcaption></figure>

<div class="note-block">Society's note: none survives. See previous interlude.</div>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/heart-symbol.PNG" class="img-light" style="width: 75px;" alt="heart mark">
<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/heart-symbol.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="heart mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"Eat slowly. Be mindful. Pastry cream is spiritual."
<cite>— chomp chomp collective, now</cite></div>

What's less disputed is that the Casa Pastéis de Belém bakery has held the "original" recipe under lock since 1837 and shows no sign of loosening its grip. The tart itself is simple in concept and unforgiving in execution: a shatteringly laminated shell holding a custard that needs scorched, blistered spots on top to be considered done at all, which means baking it hotter than most home ovens are built to comfortably reach. But you should try.

It's the inverse of how the Society claims to operate. They say they survive by leaving nothing written down; Belém has survived the opposite way, for almost two centuries, by guarding one written thing so completely that its existence functions the same way an absence would. Total secrecy and total silence turn out to look identical from the outside.

**Yield:** 12 tarts
**Prep:** 35 min • **Cook:** 20 min • **Inactive:** 30 min • **Total:** 1 hr 25 min

**For the pastry — choose one**

*Store-bought puff pastry*
- 1 sheet store-bought puff pastry, thawed if frozen
- flour, for dusting

*Homemade puff pastry*
- 2 cups (250g) all-purpose flour
- 1 teaspoon fine salt
- 2/3 cup (160ml) cold water
- 1 cup (225g) cold unsalted butter
- flour, for dusting

*Portuguese-style pastry*
- 2 cups (250g) all-purpose flour
- 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
- 2/3 cup (160ml) water
- 1 cup (225g) unsalted butter, very soft
- flour, for dusting

**For the custard:**
- 1 1/4 cups (300ml) whole milk
- 1 cinnamon stick
- 1 strip lemon peel (pith avoided)
- 1/3 cup (40g) all-purpose flour
- 1 cup (200g) granulated sugar
- 1/3 cup (80ml) water
- 4 large egg yolks
- 1 large whole egg
- 1 teaspoon vanilla extract

**Method:**

Make the pastry, if you're already committed. If using store-bought puff pastry, skip ahead to making the custard. Instructions for homemade puff pastry and Portuguese-style pastry are below.

*Homemade puff pastry*

**Combine and mix.** Combine 2 cups (250g) all-purpose flour and 1 teaspoon fine salt then mix in about 2/3 cup (160ml) cold water until a rough dough forms.

**Knead.** Briefly knead the dough until smooth, wrap, then chill for 30 minutes.

**Pound.** Pound 1 cup (225g) cold unsalted butter into a flat rectangle.

**Roll and chill.** Roll the dough large enough to enclose the butter completely, seal it inside, then roll the packet into a long rectangle and fold it in thirds like a letter. Chill for 30 minutes.

**Repeat.** Repeat the rolling and folding step five more times, chilling the dough for 20 to 30 minutes whenever it becomes soft or the butter begins to warm. Chill for at least 1 hour after the final fold before using.

**Finish the pastry.** Roll the dough to about 1/8 inch thick, then proceed to making the custard.

*Portuguese-style pastry*

**Make and knead.** Make a simple dough with 2 cups (250g) all-purpose flour, 1/2 teaspoon fine salt, and about 2/3 cup (160ml) water. Knead until smooth, cover, and rest for 20 minutes.

**Roll dough, spread butter.** Roll the dough into a very thin rectangle and spread about one-third of the 1 cup (225g) very soft unsalted butter over roughly two-thirds of its surface, leaving a narrow border.

**Fold, turn, roll, chill.** Fold the unbuttered third over the center, then fold the remaining buttered third over it, as for a letter. Turn the dough, roll it out again, and repeat twice more, using most of the remaining butter and reserving a little for the final rolling. After the final fold, roll the dough very thin, spread the reserved butter over the surface, and roll the dough tightly into a log from the short end. Wrap and chill until firm, at least 1 hour.

*Make the custard*

**Infuse the milk.** Combine 1 1/4 cups (300ml) whole milk, 1 cinnamon stick, and 1 strip lemon peel (pith avoided) in a small saucepan. Bring just to a simmer, then remove from heat and let steep for 15 minutes. Strain and discard the solids, reserving the milk.

**Make a flour slurry.** Whisk 1/3 cup (40g) all-purpose flour into about 1/4 cup of the infused milk until smooth, forming a paste with no lumps. Whisk this paste back into the remaining milk.

**Make the sugar syrup.** In a separate small saucepan, combine 1 cup (200g) granulated sugar and 1/3 cup (80ml) water. Bring to a boil and cook, without stirring, until the syrup reaches 220°F (104°C) on a candy thermometer, about 3 to 4 minutes.

**Combine.** Slowly pour the hot sugar syrup into the milk mixture, whisking constantly. Return the combined mixture to low heat and cook, whisking, until it thickens slightly, about 2 to 3 minutes. Remove from heat and let cool for 5 minutes.

**Temper in the eggs.** Whisk 4 large egg yolks, 1 large whole egg, and 1 teaspoon vanilla extract together in a bowl. Slowly whisk in about a third of the warm milk mixture to temper the eggs, then whisk this back into the remaining mixture. Strain the finished custard through a fine sieve for a silky texture. Set aside.

*Make the shells*

**Roll the pastry.** If using homemade or store-bought puff pastry, roll it on a floured surface into a tight log along its shorter side (if using a rectangular sheet, roll from the short end so the layers spiral like a cinnamon roll cross-section). Chill the log for 15 minutes, until firm enough to slice cleanly. If using Portuguese-style pastry, the dough is already rolled and chilled; proceed directly to slicing.

**Shape the shells.** Slice the log into 12 rounds, about 1 inch thick. Working one at a time, press each round into the base and up the sides of a pastel de nata tin or muffin tin cavity, spiral side down, pushing the layers up the walls with your thumbs until the shell is thin and even, slightly higher than the rim.

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/pasteis%20de%20nata%20-%20nata%20thumbing.PNG"><figcaption>Shape the shells</figcaption></figure>

*Bake*

**Fill and bake.** Preheat the oven as hot as it will go, ideally 500°F (260°C), with a rack in the upper third. Divide the custard evenly among the shells, filling about 3/4 full. Bake for 12 to 15 minutes, watching closely near the end, until the tops are deeply blistered with dark, near-black spots and the pastry edges are a deep golden brown. The scorched spots are correct. A pastel de nata with a pale, even top has not been baked hot enough.

**Cool briefly.** Let cool in the tin for 5 minutes before removing. Best eaten warm, within a few hours, with a dusting of cinnamon or powdered sugar if you like, though purists take theirs plain.

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on choosing pastry:</strong> Store-bought puff pastry works very well here and is the basis for the recipe as written: it gives you a crisp, flaky, well-laminated shell without turning the pastry itself into a project. Homemade puff pastry does the same job with the satisfaction, or burden, of having made the layers yourself. For something closer to the pastry traditionally used for pastéis de nata, make the Portuguese-style pastry. Rather than enclosing a block of butter and repeatedly folding the dough as in conventional puff pastry, butter is spread directly over the dough as it is folded and rolled. The finished dough is rolled into a tight log, deliberately preserving the spiral so those concentric layers become the crisp, curled shell around the custard. All three work. Depends on your commitment to the cause.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on time:</strong> The prep and total times given for this recipe assume store-bought puff pastry. Making conventional puff pastry from scratch adds roughly 3 1/2 to 4 hours, much of it chilling rather than active work. Portuguese-style pastry adds about 2 to 2 1/2 hours, again mostly resting and chilling. Neither requires several hours of continuous labor, but both require you to start considerably earlier. Store-bought puff pastry requires you to remember to thaw it. There are arguments for progress.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on using tins:</strong> Traditional pastel de nata tins are shallow, slightly flared, and considerably wider at the top than the cavities of a standard muffin tin. If you have them, use them. Press the pastry thinly across the bottom and up the sides, fill each shell about 3/4 full, and bake as directed. The wider, shallower shape exposes more custard to the oven's heat and gives you more of the blistered top and crisp laminated shell that define the tart. Depending on the size of your tins, the recipe may make slightly more or fewer than 12.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the heat:</strong> If your oven won't reach 500°F (260°C), get it as close as it will go and extend the baking time slightly, accepting that the blister pattern will be gentler. There is no good substitute for real heat here. This is one of the few places in this book where the technique really does only work one way.</div>

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/pasteis%20de%20nata%20-%20nata%20tins.PNG"><figcaption>Pastel de nata tins</figcaption></figure>

Not Pastéis de Belém, but hey, beggars can't be choosers. So creamy, custardy, tender. Later I ended up in a Lisbon thrift store where I found special tins to make them in for like .40€ a piece. ## XI. Chocolate Roll

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/roll-hero.PNG"><figcaption>Chocolate Roll</figcaption></figure>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending.png" class="img-light" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">
<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending-dark.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"We never named it. Some rolled chocolate with me. Others unrolled the names."
<cite>— ledger fragment, undated, signed with an X mark</cite></div>

There is no evidence the Swiss roll has anything to do with Switzerland. There is also no evidence the brazo de gitano has anything to do with an arm, or that the Japanese roll cake came from Japan. A thin sponge, baked fast, rolled warm, filled with something white and cold: this might be the most renamed thing in this book, and every culture that got hold of it named it after somewhere else. Didn't even have to be their own home.

The version here is the one I actually think about when someone says the words, which is not the jam-filled English tea version (also kind of known as the Swiss version). It is chocolate sponge, white cream, chocolate on the outside. Several billions of the industrial snack-cake version have been sold. Neither you nor I are above those. Stop lying. What follows is that object, made from a genoise. The technique underneath it is the only whipped-egg foam in this book: no chemical leavening, no fat to speak of, nothing holding the sponge up but air you put there on purpose and did not knock back out. Roll it warm or it will crack. That rule has no exceptions and no local variant.

**Yield:** one 10-inch roll, 8 to 10 slices
**Prep:** 35 min • **Cook:** 10 min • **Inactive:** 1 hr 45 min • **Total:** 2 hr 30 min

**For the sponge:**
- 4 large eggs, room temperature
- 2/3 cup (135g) granulated sugar
- 1/2 cup (65g) all-purpose flour
- 1/4 cup (25g) unsweetened Dutch-process cocoa powder
- pinch of salt
- 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
- powdered sugar, for the towel

**For the cream filling:**
- 4 ounces (113g) cream cheese, softened
- 1/3 cup (40g) powdered sugar
- 1 cup (240ml) cold heavy cream
- 1 teaspoon vanilla extract

**For the glaze:**
- 8 ounces (227g) semisweet chocolate, finely chopped
- 1/2 cup (120ml) heavy cream
- 1 tablespoon (15ml) neutral oil or light corn syrup

**Method:**

1. **Preheat and prep.** Heat the oven to 425°F (220°C). Line a 10x15-inch jelly roll pan with parchment, leaving overhang on the short sides.

2. **Whip the eggs and sugar.** In a heatproof bowl set over barely simmering water, whisk the eggs and sugar until just warm to the touch. Remove from heat and beat on high until tripled in volume, pale, and thick enough to hold a ribbon on the surface for a few seconds, about 5 to 7 minutes.

3. **Fold in the dry ingredients.** Sift the flour, cocoa, and salt over the foam in two additions, folding gently each time just until no streaks remain. Fold in the vanilla. Cocoa deflates a foam faster than flour alone does, so fold with more conviction and fewer strokes than you would for a plain sponge.

4. **Bake.** Spread evenly into the pan. Bake 7 to 9 minutes, until the top springs back and the edges just pull from the sides. Do not overbake. A dry sponge cracks instead of rolling, and there is no recovering it afterward.

5. **Roll while warm.** Dust a clean kitchen towel generously with powdered sugar. Immediately invert the hot cake onto it and peel off the parchment. You can sprinkle powdered sugar directly onto the roll, too. Starting from a short end, roll the cake up inside the towel and let it cool completely, still rolled, seam-side down. It is learning the shape while it is still willing to.

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/chocolate%20roll%20-%20towel%20rolling.PNG"><figcaption>Rolling</figcaption></figure>

6. **Make the filling.** Beat the cream cheese and powdered sugar until completely smooth with no lumps. With the mixer running, stream in the cold cream and vanilla, then whip to firm peaks. The cream cheese is doing structural work here, not flavor work: plain whipped cream weeps into the sponge within a day and this doesn't.

7. **Fill and re-roll.** Gently unroll the cooled cake. Spread the filling evenly, leaving a half-inch border along the far edge. Re-roll without the towel, using it only to guide. Wrap and chill at least 1 hour, until firm enough to hold a clean edge.

8. **Glaze.** Bring the cream just to a simmer and pour it over the chopped chocolate. Let stand 2 minutes, then stir smooth from the center outward. Stir in the oil. Let cool to barely warm, about 90°F (32°C), thick enough to coat but still pourable.

9. **Coat and set.** Trim both ends of the chilled roll with a warm knife, wiping between cuts. Set the roll on a rack over a sheet pan and pour the glaze along its length, letting it run down and cover the sides. Chill 20 minutes to set. Slice cold, wiping the knife between every cut.

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the crack:</strong> a little cracking along the outside is normal and not a failure. Bakeries pipe cream over the seams to hide it. Here the glaze does that job for you, and might be the whole reason the coated version exists.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the glaze temperature:</strong> too warm and it runs off and pools; too cool and it sets in ridges as it moves. Ninety degrees (32°C), poured in one pass, no going back over it with a spatula.</div>

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the ends:</strong> the trimmed ends are the baker's, immediately, standing at the counter. This is not part of any local tradition I've found, so I'm establishing it.</div>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending-dark.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"The baker always has rights to the remainders. These are not scraps."
<cite>— ledger fragment, marginal note, deep red ink, signed "Always"</cite></div>

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/roll-coda.PNG"><figcaption>Chocolate Roll</figcaption></figure>

You know, the thin chocolate layer coating the outside, with chocolate cake rolled up with white cream on the inside. In plastic wrappers. Mass produced by enormous bakeries. Sweet staple of youth. They used to sell a brand of snacks called Lady Linda at my corner bodega. Their Swiss Cake Rolls were basically on par with Little Debbie, but the name seemed a little more adult. ## XII. Moon Pie

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/Moon%20Pie.jpeg"><figcaption>Moon Pie</figcaption></figure>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending.png" class="img-light" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">
<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending-dark.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"Moonpie moonpie."
<cite>— Dad, every time he brought home a double-decker for me</cite></div>

The moon pie's documented origin is a Chattanooga Bakery salesman who, in 1917, asked a group of Tennessee coal miners what they wanted in a snack, and got back a request for something as big as the moon they could see rising over the mine. What followed, two graham cookies sandwiching marshmallow filling, the whole thing dipped in chocolate, became a fixture of Southern working kitchens and general stores long before it became a Mardi Gras throw, tossed from floats in Mobile alongside the beads. That's the closing loop this book owes you: Mobile again, the same city that got the Mardi Gras credit line straightened out back in the king cake chapter, still catching a pastry other places get to claim first.

**Yield:** 8 moon pies
**Prep:** 45 min • **Cook:** 25 min • **Inactive:** 1 hr 20 min • **Total:** 2 hr 30 min

**For the cookies:**
- 1 3/4 cups (220g) all-purpose flour
- 1/2 cup (60g) graham flour (or substitute more all-purpose)
- 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
- 1/2 teaspoon salt
- 1/2 cup (113g) unsalted butter, softened
- 1/3 cup (65g) packed brown sugar
- 1/4 cup (85g) honey
- 1 large egg
- 1 teaspoon vanilla extract

**For the marshmallow filling:**
- 1 tablespoon (9g) unflavored gelatin
- 1/4 cup (60ml) cold water
- 1 cup (200g) granulated sugar
- 1/3 cup (110g) light corn syrup
- 1/4 cup (60ml) water
- pinch of salt
- 1 teaspoon vanilla extract

**For the coating:**
- 12 ounces (340g) semisweet or dark chocolate, chopped
- 2 teaspoons neutral oil or coconut oil

**Method:**

1. **Make the cookie dough.** Whisk together the all-purpose flour, graham flour, baking soda, and salt. In a separate bowl, cream the butter, brown sugar, and honey until light. Beat in the egg and vanilla, then mix in the dry ingredients just until combined. Wrap and chill the dough for 30 minutes.

2. **Roll and cut.** Preheat the oven to 350°F (180°C). Roll the chilled dough to about 1/4 inch thick on a floured surface. Cut into 16 rounds, about 3 inches across, and transfer to parchment-lined baking sheets.

3. **Bake.** Bake for 10 to 12 minutes, until the edges are just set and lightly golden. The cookies will firm up further as they cool. Cool completely on a rack.

4. **Bloom the gelatin.** Sprinkle the gelatin over the 1/4 cup cold water in a small bowl and let sit for 5 minutes to soften.

5. **Make the sugar syrup.** Combine the granulated sugar, corn syrup, 1/4 cup water, and salt in a saucepan. Bring to a boil and cook until it reaches 240°F (116°C) on a candy thermometer, soft-ball stage.

6. **Whip the marshmallow.** With a stand mixer running on low, pour the softened gelatin into the mixer bowl, then slowly stream in the hot sugar syrup down the side of the bowl, avoiding the whisk. Increase speed to high and whip until thick, glossy, and roughly tripled in volume, about 8 to 10 minutes. Add the vanilla in the last minute of whipping. The mixture should hold a soft peak and be difficult to pour, more like batter than syrup.

7. **Assemble.** Working quickly before the marshmallow sets, spread or pipe a generous layer onto the flat side of half the cooled cookies. Top each with a second cookie, flat side down, pressing gently to spread the filling to the edges. Let sit at room temperature for at least 30 minutes to firm up.

8. **Melt the chocolate.** Melt the chocolate and oil together in a heatproof bowl over barely simmering water, or in short bursts in the microwave, stirring until smooth.

9. **Coat.** Dip each assembled pie into the melted chocolate, using a fork to lift it out and let excess drip away, then set on a parchment-lined tray. Chill until the chocolate sets, about 15 minutes.

<div class="note-block"><strong>Note on the filling:</strong> if the marshmallow starts setting before you've assembled all the pies, a few seconds of gentle warmth from a kitchen torch waved over the surface will loosen it enough to keep working. Do not walk away and come back to it. Homemade marshmallow does not wait.</div>

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/pie2.png"><figcaption>Moon Pie</figcaption></figure>

<div class="marginalia">I never knew that Moon Pie's brand name is actually MoonPie. Makes sense. Growing up that's how people around me pronounced it. Moonpie moonpie, my dad would say. And I didn't know why he said it like that. Maybe he didn't either. I used to get the big ones at the gas station, the double deckers: more moon, more pie, more marshmallow. But when I went to my first Mardi Gras parades after moving to Mobile, AL, I found out all they threw were these dinky little foil-wrapped "moon pies" to the revelers. Branded by the Mystic Society, of course. They were tiny tiny. But better than NOLA where they didn't even throw them. And they didn't have Societies either. My last parade I caught about a gallon bucket worth of beads, three doubloons, and twelve moon pies. Can't remember the name of the Society, but with the Spanish historical influence in Mobile, it was probably Catalan.</div>

## Marginalia: Why Alabama?

I know you've noticed the pattern, but I've also noticed that I keep noticing it. Alabama. Like when discussing King Cake, the earliest Mardi Gras credit goes to Mobile, not New Orleans. And millions of Moon Pies get thrown from Mobile's Mardi Gras floats every year, same as beads. Even the chapter on baklava couldn't resist threading back to Alabama. I even know of a xuxo origin story that runs through Alabama, in that version the Twelve don't just happen to be nearby, they walk out of someone's kitchen with the thing, invent an acrobat and a sneeze to cover the trail, and hand the whole story to Girona instead. Not credit. The opposite of credit, proof they'd rather manufacture someone else's myth than let anyone trace the real one back to them, and pointedly not back to Alabama either, which is the one place with enough of a paper trail to actually get them caught. But without the xuxo, where would the Catalan Twelve even be? The same place, to be honest. It's The Twelve no matter.

So, yes, I'd like to tell you the Alabama fingerprints showed up as a rigorous cross-referencing of guild records and ledger fragments. They didn't. Obviously they're mine; I'm from Alabama, and once you notice you're from somewhere, you start seeing it everywhere, whether or not it's actually there.

I grew up in Alabama. Not exclusively, my family was spread up and down the Gulf Coast, and I've got roots in more than one of the port towns this book keeps almost-implicating. But Alabama is where I spent the most time, the years that make you who you end up being before you've had any say in the matter. I learned a lot there, and in a way that matters. I didn't pick up every recipe there. What I picked up was a posture toward food: patience, respect for cold fat, not rushing a dough. I've said as much elsewhere in this book without saying where.

Then I left, the way people from formative places do. You don't stop being from somewhere. You just stop being there.

I don't actually think I'm wrong that something passed through there. Ports absorb things. People move through them and leave a little of wherever they came from, and take a little of wherever they land. Like, Mobile had Mardi Gras before New Orleans did. That's not folklore, that's just true. Whether a closed, unnamed dozen of traveling bakers also passed through at some point is a much bigger claim, and I'm not making it, not seriously. But I'm not not making it either.

So the joke, if it is one, was never really about Alabama somehow being secretly behind everything. Alabama is not the joke. Maybe it's that I'm the joke, at least in the sense that a writer can't help but drag their own hometown into every story they tell, whether the facts support it or not. In the sense that a baker can't help but do the same. I've dressed this up as a Society-tracking exercise. Underneath, it's just someone who grew up on the Gulf Coast, insisting, gently and repeatedly, with his heart on his sleeve, that the place mattered. And I'm still tracking the Society, Alabama-grounded or not.

## Conclusion: On the Matter of Twelve

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/twelvetally.png"><figcaption>A recount, mid-count</figcaption></figure>

<div class="pullquote">"Twelve? Because twelve is never questioned. No one asks why a dozen."
<cite>— ledger fragment, undated</cite></div>

I asked why a dozen. I found no answers down the rabbit hole, only riddles, dead ends, and false starters for sourdough.

And it became known in some circles that I was putting together a reconstruction of fragments of the ledger. Someone reached out. Someone claiming to be a member of the Society. Felt real enough, or at least why not, so I wrote back. I've included the exchange after this.

Here is my postulate: the Society cannot be the same twelve people the whole time. Nobody's craft lasts three hundred years on twelve sets of hands. Someone has been leaving. Someone has been arriving to replace them. I can't imagine that the person filling an open seat ever fully understands what they've joined. But that's what they're all about: maintaining tradition, and creating it, at the same time, in the same motion.

My guess as to how they do it without a trace: whatever gets handed down gets handed down without paper. A recipe you write out survives its author and starts belonging to whoever reads it next, which is exactly the kind of ownership this group seems constitutionally averse to. A recipe you teach hand over hand, mouth to ear, in a kitchen, dies with the person who never wrote it down, unless they've already put it into someone else's hands first. I've come to think that's not a failure of record-keeping. It might be the entire method.

What that leaves behind isn't a family tree. It's fingerprints. A monastery refectory ledger logs "twelve hands at the ovens" for a feast one year, no names attached, phrased more like a headcount than a credit. A guild record from a Gulf port lists a due paid by "the dozen," undifferentiated, in a year that sits uncomfortably close to one of the gaps in this book's own ledger. A photograph, more recent and far more obviously explicable and yet still uncaptioned, shows twelve people behind a counter that looks a great deal like the hotel kitchen from the second interlude's 1965 fragment, none of them named, all of them looking entirely used to that.

None of these fingerprints match each other in style or language or century. That's either evidence they belong to the same unbroken thread, or evidence of nothing at all, twelve unrelated dozens who happened to share a number. I want them to be the same thread. What I want doesn't matter. Things are or they aren't. Only, the twelve are. Though they'd say they might not be.

Since I just confessed that my attachment to the South didn't end when I left, another correction is owed: the Society didn't begin in Catalonia. It's just where they were first recorded doing their work, if that's what it's called. That's a different claim than an origin, the same way the first written record of a word isn't the moment someone invented it. Everything before that first sighting is unrecorded, not un-happened.

So, why twelve? In the case of this book, there are twelve recipes merely because there are twelve members in the Society. In the end, it's just a headcount, not a code. Everything else I'm still working out.

What I can tell you with more confidence is what these twelve recipes have in common, once you strip away the Society and the marginalia and the half-legible names. Cold fat handled quickly. Custard that wants gentle heat and no shortcuts. A dough that tells you when it's ready if you're willing to stop kneading and listen instead of watching a clock. None of that is mysterious. All of it is learnable, and none of it requires believing in anything you can't taste.

The Twelve, if they exist, don't need this book. They never needed credit and they're not going to start wanting it now because I strung together found marginalia and drew conclusions about the Gulf Coast. What this book is actually for is simpler: twelve techniques worth having, from twelve places that all, at some point, insisted their version was the real one. Make enough of them and you start to notice they were all half right.

Go make the xuxos again. They're better the second time. Or go make something new. Hands that wait do too.

## Correspondence

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/letters.png"><figcaption>For you to decide</figcaption></figure>

I said earlier that someone reached out, and that I'd include the communication in full. Here it is, four letters, two exchanges, possibly misses.

The contact came through a person who introduced himself only as Crumb. It had to be the same name I'd run into before down in the rabbit holes. Hildevart Crumb. He'd popped up in fifteenth-century baking lore about a divide between biscuits and cookies and what could and should be called what. I'd also seen him at a regional fair in 1261 causing a ruckus about pastry terminology. I didn't want to twist knots in time, so I just asked him whether he was older than he looked. He didn't deny it. He also didn't confirm it. He just said the name gets easier to carry the longer you've had practice not answering for it.

What follows is the correspondence in the order received/sent, letters passed through Crumb's hands in both directions, in whatever order he chose to deliver them, which I have no way of verifying was the order they were written. I'm not even sure we were responding to each other's previous one. They were cryptic, and that's par.

<div class="section-break">* * *</div>

<div class="letter-label">Letter One</div>

You've been reaching out. Since before you even heard of us. We heard you. Your cookies. Your biscuits. Your cakes. Your tortas. You are closer now than you've ever been, yet further. You are clever, and you're finding the truth on your own. It is our truth. You've known this but now you need to know this.

Now we're reaching out to you. This is how this reached you. Not because you asked loud enough, in enough places, but because we had been reading your own ledger, while you were trying to decipher ours. It was easier to reach out to you than to keep pretending we hadn't seen. Hadn't heard.

Names don't matter. Like mine. Crumb will tell you that. He understands that. He's tried to make his matter for a long time. No matter, the fewer people who can be asked, the more people who can answer wrong. Don't ask.

Crumb is a door, not a room. Not one of us.

I will bake something for you, so you know this isn't a trick someone's playing on you. We don't like trickery, although we have found it can serve a purpose, just ask your new friend Crumb. Sorry, I won't bake for you, but I'll tell you where you're wrong. In this ledger of yours, you used cold Crisco for the biscuits. Some of us have always insisted on lard, cold as it comes, others butter, and the argument over which is correct has raged for eons. You wrote as much yourself, or close. And you're wrong. People who claim to know have enemies. We do. I'm on the lard side, making mortal foes. You will make them, too. One day. We set the standards. It may take another 100 years, but then we will have no enemies on the lard question. Yet we always will because we always have. A cycle.

You will ask about Catalonia. Your version is good enough. Stop wasting time.

You will ask how many. Twelve is the answer you already have. Learn to be satisfied with counts already: headcounts, recipe counts, counting down the minutes, counting up the years.

You will ask what we saw in your ledger. You have pieces of the old ledger, the one you keep quoting like scripture. There is likely another like it. No. Likely not. Not like it. Nothing is complete. It may never be finished, that isn't the kind of thing that gets finished. What you have been building is already somewhere in it. Or maybe pieces of it are already somewhere in yours.

Ask what you like. Someone will read the question, but there are no promises that you'll receive a response to the one you asked.

Ask real questions and you might get real answers.

<div class="section-break">* * *</div>

<div class="letter-label">Letter Two</div>

To whoever wrote to me, or to whoever reads this,

I'm not sure how I feel about you reading my "ledger." I'm writing a cookbook. Am I only seeing fragments of the Society's lost cookbook? You already know my recipes. I'm sure you do. Still, what did you see?

Yes, I've been searching for the Catalan Twelve. Twelve recipes based on myths and rumors and fragments. Is that you? Or a collective You? I don't know whether you reading my book before I've finished is closer to being edited or being audited, and I know you know that's an important difference. I just want to get it right.

I'll take the correction on the biscuits. It's now in a note, lard: cold - as you insist. Go back and read it. You already know it. I don't think anyone is wrong so much as loyal to different things. I don't like to argue.

You say Crumb isn't one of you. That's an answer, but it's a strange one. You chose him to carry these letters. Why use a door you seem so eager to insist isn't part of the house?

You told me to ask real questions, so here are some:

1. What's the best method for being consistent across one's own baking and technique?
2. Why does a knot hold more heat than a spiral? Why is this a secret? It's not a secret. I can't remember who taught me though.
3. Why is your society "secret?"
4. Personal curiosity: the guild record I found, the one that logs a due paid by "the dozen" at a Gulf port, undifferentiated, no names. Did I grow up there? You obviously know enough about me to know where that is.

I look forward to your reply.

<div class="section-break">* * *</div>

<div class="letter-label">Letter Three</div>

Dear Narrator:

You asked about Crumb first. We didn't. We told you what he is because you keep trying to make him something else. A door is useful because it opens. It doesn't have to own the house. Neither does the person standing in it.

I. You ask for the best method. There isn't one. There's only the same one, over and over: hands that wait. A person who is paying attention will feel when dough is ready the way you feel when a conversation has gone on long enough. Consistency doesn't come from matching one recipe to the next. It comes from matching the waiting, every time, regardless of what's in the bowl. That's the whole technique. I'm sorry it isn't more complicated. People want it to be more complicated.

II. The knot holds more heat than the spiral because it presses more surface against itself, less exposed to air, more exposed to itself. It's physics, not secrecy. Yes, one of us wrote "secret" in the ledger. This is errata now. The physics behind the heat weren't understood. We'd merely waited with the spiral and the knot and learned. We taught. Kept the "secret" to ourselves and our students. We never saw the use in explaining what anyone could learn by touching the two side by side, but that's not hiding. We thought people would find this on their own. But people are too impatient and they don't wait. So they didn't learn. That is technique, which is obviously not the same as secrecy. Ask yourself: have you ever hidden something on purpose, or only failed to mention it because no one asked the right question at the right time. Or perhaps you failed to... accidentally on purpose.

III. You keep calling us secret. We would call ourselves private, if we called ourselves anything, which we mostly don't. A secret implies effort. We have made none. But you did. You made the effort. You looked loudly. You travelled stompingly. You baked patiently. You've written ledgerly. So we found you. Because you looked. Most people don't look. That isn't our doing.

IV. And the port. You thought I would name it? I could, but it's not what you need to know. What I will tell you: the ledger doesn't record names, it records ideas, places, pauses, and none of those move the way people do. If you have a past there, you already knew before you asked the question. If you don't, nothing I say will put one there. I'm not in the business of handing people a history they didn't already have some part of. Ask your family. They know. Or don't.

You wanted knowledge. I cannot answer for you. But I have answered you.

<div class="section-break">* * *</div>

<div class="letter-label">Letter Four</div>

Dear ???

You said to ask my family. I haven't. Not yet. I'm not sure if I will.

Tell me if I'm warm: maybe one doesn't join the Society. Maybe one becomes. Or, like family, like heritage, you're already a part of it and it's already a part of you.

Actually, I don't know that I need a response to that. I think I'd trust your silence more than an explicit answer.

I'll wait.

[No reply came.]

## A Baker's Dozen

<figure><img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/Tea.PNG"><figcaption>XIII</figcaption></figure>

<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending.png" class="img-light" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">
<img src="https://ik.imagekit.io/chompchomp/ending-dark.PNG" class="img-dark" style="width: 75px;" alt="society mark">

<div class="pullquote tight" style="--tight-margin-top:10px">"The thirteenth was never a gift. It's safety."
<cite>— Society's note, in my mailbox, attribution unreliable</cite></div>

<div class="section-break">* * *</div>

After sending that last letter, I waited, not with bated breath, just with my thoughts, totally believing I would never get a reply or ever see Crumb again. He showed up at my apartment around 6 am one morning looking a little tired and a lot disheveled. He handed me a folded piece of paper, not in an envelope.

"Here, you might find this interesting."

"What is it?" I took it.

"Something I picked up during my travels. Something I'd been searching for, for a long time. It's kind of a genre-bending recipe. It's both a cookie and a biscuit, and it's called a cake."

I opened the paper; it was a recipe for a Southern Tea Cake.

"This could go in your book. A thirteenth recipe, make your baker's dozen."

He turned to leave.

"Wait! Are you Hildevart Crumb?"

"I think so." He walked off.

That's the last I saw of him. I went inside and checked my pantry. I had all the ingredients, so I tried my last recipe.

It doesn't count as one of the twelve. I want to be clear about that before I say anything else about it, because the whole point of a baker's dozen, historically, wasn't generosity. It was insurance: bakers in medieval England padded their orders with an extra loaf because the penalty for shorting a customer, even by accident, could cost them a fine they couldn't afford. An extra loaf was cheaper than a mistake. This is that extra loaf. It sits outside the count on purpose, the same way it's always sat outside every other count.

If this recipe belongs to anyone in this book's mythology, it belongs to whichever version of "Crumb" wrote it, a name I've already spent a full section of this book failing to pin to a single person or century. Crumb's whole documented career, real and embellished both, seems to circle one unresolved argument: what separates a biscuit from a cookie, and whether the distinction survives translation at all. He's on record calling the same pastry two different names in two different towns and walking away from the dispute both times. A Southern tea cake is, as far as I can tell, the dish that finally beat him at his own game, because it refuses to resolve the argument even when you ask it directly. It's called a cake. It's shelved as a cookie. It's made and eaten like a soft, barely sweet biscuit, hand-shaped rather than rolled, no lamination, no rise to speak of. Whoever handed this to me clearly thought that was funny. I think it might be the closest thing to a joke this entire correspondence ever told.

I want to say, though, that the joke is not the whole story, and I'd be doing this dish a disservice if I let the taxonomy trivia carry the chapter. Tea cakes have a real, documented history in the American South that predates any of this book's games with names: they were made by enslaved cooks, often for the households that enslaved them, and likely out of the plain, shelf-stable ingredients a Southern pantry could reliably keep on hand. That history doesn't disappear because a fictional baker found the recipe taxonomically amusing four hundred years later. Whatever their initial origin, Southern tea cakes have endured because of the generations of Black Southern cooks who have made and passed them down. This deserves to be said outright rather than folded quietly into a footnote.

I grew up eating something close to these, plainer than what follows below, made by people who never once called them anything but tea cakes and never explained why. I didn't ask the right questions until long after I could have asked them to the people who'd have known the real answers. That's a different regret than anything else in this book, and not one I'm going to try to resolve here.

**Yield:** about 20 tea cakes
**Prep:** 20 min • **Cook:** 12 min • **Inactive:** 28 min • **Total:** 1 hr

**Ingredients:**
- 2 1/2 cups (315g) all-purpose flour
- 1 teaspoon baking powder
- 1/4 teaspoon baking soda
- 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
- 1/2 cup (113g) unsalted butter, softened, or 1/4 cup butter + 1/4 cup shortening for a softer, more traditional texture
- 2/3 cup (135g) granulated sugar
- 1 large egg
- 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
- 1/3 cup (80ml) buttermilk

**Method:**
1. **Cream the butter and sugar.** Beat until light and pale, about 3 minutes.
2. **Add the egg and vanilla.** Beat in until fully combined.
3. **Combine the dry ingredients separately.** Whisk together the flour, baking powder, baking soda, and salt.
4. **Alternate dry and wet.** Add the dry ingredients in two additions, alternating with the buttermilk, mixing just until combined. The dough will be soft and slightly sticky.
5. **Rest the dough.** Cover and chill 20 to 30 minutes, just enough to make it manageable by hand.
6. **Shape by hand.** Scoop by rounded tablespoons, roll gently into a ball between your palms, then flatten each one with your palm, not a glass, not a rolling pin, into a rough, uneven disc a bit less than half an inch thick. The imperfection is the point.
7. **Bake.** Bake at 350°F (180°C) for 10 to 12 minutes, until the edges just turn golden. Centers should look slightly underdone; they firm up as they cool.
8. **Cool.** 5 minutes on the sheet before transferring to a rack.

## Back Cover

<p class="lead">No one can prove the Catalan Twelve exist. That has never stopped them from taking credit. Or avoiding it.</p>

For centuries, a single dough technique, a stubborn insistence on cold butter, a habit of hiding something valuable inside a ring of bread, has turned up in kitchens with no business knowing each other's business. Girona has a pastry. So does Mobile. A hotel in Lancashire has a pudding no one can trace, and a Chattanooga bakery has a snack named for the sky. This book collects twelve of those recipes, real and fully tested, alongside whatever fragments of the ledger survived long enough to be reprinted here.

Make the xuxo. Make the biscuits. Make the moon pie last, so the loop closes properly. Believe as much of the rest as you'd like.
