On Mug Cakes, or, Preparation for a Demo: Recipes, Notes, and Histories

RECIPES

Chocolate Mug Cake

  • 4 tbsp all-purpose flour
  • 4 tbsp sugar
  • 2 tbsp cocoa powder
  • 1 egg | vegan: 3 tbsp aquafaba or 1 tbsp flax meal + 3 tbsp water
  • 3 tbsp milk | vegan: oat, almond, or soy milk
  • 3 tbsp neutral oil
  • Pinch of salt
  • Optional: a few chocolate chips pressed into the center | vegan: use dairy-free chips

Add wet ingredients to a 12oz+ mug and mix. Add dry ingredients and stir until smooth. Microwave 60–90 seconds.


Vanilla / Yellow Mug Cake

  • 4 tbsp all-purpose flour
  • 3 tbsp sugar
  • 1/4 tsp baking powder
  • 1 egg yolk | vegan: 3 tbsp aquafaba or 1 tbsp flax meal + 3 tbsp water
  • 3 tbsp milk | vegan: oat, almond, or soy milk
  • 2 tbsp butter, melted | vegan: melted coconut oil, vegan butter, or neutral oil
  • 1/4 tsp vanilla extract
  • Pinch of salt

Add wet ingredients to mug and mix. Add dry ingredients and stir until smooth. Microwave 60–90 seconds.


Lemon Mug Cake

  • 4 tbsp all-purpose flour
  • 3 tbsp sugar
  • 1/4 tsp baking powder
  • 1 egg yolk | vegan: 3 tbsp aquafaba preferred, won't compete with lemon
  • 2 tbsp milk | vegan: oat, almond, or soy milk
  • 2 tbsp butter, melted | vegan: melted coconut oil, vegan butter, or neutral oil
  • 1 tbsp fresh lemon juice
  • 1 tsp lemon zest
  • Pinch of salt

Add wet ingredients to mug and mix. Add dry ingredients and stir until smooth. Microwave 60–90 seconds.


Nutella Mug Cake

  • 4 tbsp Nutella | vegan: Justin's Chocolate Hazelnut Butter or Nocciolata Dairy-Free
  • 1 egg | vegan: 3 tbsp aquafaba
  • 2 tbsp all-purpose flour

Add egg (or aquafaba) to mug and beat lightly. Add Nutella and mix together, then add flour and stir until smooth. Microwave 60–75 seconds. Don't overbake.

Tip: Warm the open Nutella jar in the microwave for 20–30 seconds and stir before measuring — it becomes much easier to work with and stays fluid throughout any baking session.


Matcha Mug Cake

  • 4 tbsp all-purpose flour
  • 3 tbsp sugar
  • 1-1/2 tsp culinary-grade matcha powder
  • 1/4 tsp baking powder
  • 1 egg yolk | vegan: 3 tbsp aquafaba or 1 tbsp flax meal + 3 tbsp water
  • 3 tbsp milk | vegan: oat milk recommended — neutral and works well with matcha
  • 2 tbsp butter, melted | vegan: melted coconut oil — pairs well with matcha
  • Pinch of salt

Add wet ingredients to mug and mix. Add dry ingredients and stir until smooth. Microwave 60–90 seconds.

Tip: 3 tbsp milk can also work in place of butter. Whole milk preferred over oat — better body, matcha flavor comes through cleaner. Oat still works fine for vegan preparation.

NOTES

How Mug Cakes Work At All

The microwave heats water molecules in the batter rapidly, creating steam that sets the cake from the inside out — opposite of a conventional oven. That's why they cook in under 2 minutes but can go rubbery fast if overdone. You're looking for just-set, not fully dry.

The center will look slightly underdone when you pull it out. That's correct. It continues cooking for about 30 seconds after you stop the microwave.

No oven spring, no crust — the texture is denser and moister than a baked cake by design, not by accident.


Wet Ingredients First

In a small mug there's not enough room to whisk dry ingredients properly — flour especially clumps in the corners. Adding wet first gives you a mixing base; dry folds in more evenly. Same principle as adding flour to creamed butter in a full cake — you're incorporating into something that's already moving.


Eggs And What They Do

In these recipes the egg provides moisture, fat, and light binding — not structural lift like in a layer cake. That's why substitutes work reasonably well here; the egg doesn't have to do as much.


Aquafaba As An Egg Substitute

The liquid from canned chickpeas — proteins and starches that mimic egg white behavior. Works here because the egg isn't doing heavy lifting. Use 3 tbsp per egg or yolk. Neutral flavor, no bean taste in the finished cake.

Flax egg (1 tbsp flax meal + 3 tbsp water, rested 5 min) is the other option — slightly nuttier, good with chocolate and matcha.


Butter vs. Oil

Butter adds flavor and a little structure; oil adds pure moisture with no flavor. In a mug cake context oil is almost always fine because the other flavors — cocoa, matcha, Nutella, lemon — are doing the heavy lifting.

Melted coconut oil is a good vegan butter swap — neutral to slightly sweet, pairs particularly well with matcha.


Milk Swaps

Oat milk is the most neutral plant milk — closest to whole milk in body and behavior. Almond and soy both work; soy has the most protein which makes it slightly better structurally. Whole milk is still the best option for matcha specifically — the fat carries the flavor better.


The Nutella Situation

Three ingredients for thus recipe because Nutella is already fat + sugar + chocolate flavor in one jar. Warming the jar (lid off, 20-30 seconds) makes it fluid and cooperative — worth doing before the session starts.

Wet first matters most here: beat the egg in the mug first, then add Nutella, then flour.


Gluten-Free

Almond and oat flour substitute 1:1 by volume — no math required. Both tend to cook slightly faster; start checking at 60 seconds. Coconut flour is the exception — absorbs dramatically more liquid, not worth the trouble here. 1:1 blends (Bob's Red Mill, King Arthur) are the most reliable if someone has a bag at home.


Almond Flour

Same volume as all-purpose. Slightly denser and moister with a faint nuttiness. Works especially well with chocolate, Nutella, and matcha. Start checking at 60 seconds — tends to finish faster than all-purpose.


Oat Flour

Same volume as all-purpose. Neutral flavor, closest to all-purpose in texture and timing. Easy to make at home: blend rolled oats until fine. Use certified GF oats if celiac is a concern. Start checking at 60 seconds.


Coconut Flour

Not recommended for straight substitution. Use 1 tbsp per 4 tbsp all-purpose and add an extra tbsp of milk. More trouble than it's worth in a mug cake context.


1:1 GF Blends

Reliable and convenient. Use same volume as all-purpose. May produce a slightly gummier texture. Start checking at 75 seconds and add time in 10-second increments. Easy to overshoot.


Microwave Timing and Variables

Every microwave is different — wattage varies widely. 60 seconds is always your starting point; add in 10-second increments. Underbaked is recoverable (put it back in); overbaked is a hockey puck.

Ceramic mugs work best — thin ones heat unevenly, metal is obviously out. 12oz minimum mug size; the batter rises and needs headroom.


Toppings and Finishing

A pinch of flaky salt on top of chocolate or Nutella right after it comes out is excellent. Powdered sugar on lemon or vanilla. A small scoop of ice cream turns it into an actual dessert.

The chocolate chip center trick: press a few chips into the batter before microwaving — you get a molten moment in the middle.

HISTORIES

A Brief History of the Microwave Oven

In 1945, Raytheon engineer Percy Spencer was testing an active radar magnetron tube when he noticed the candy bar in his pocket had melted. Rather than change his pants, he investigated. The result was a patent filed that same year.

The first commercial microwave, the Radarange, arrived in 1947 — roughly six feet tall, 750 pounds, and priced at around $5,000. It was marketed to restaurants and industrial kitchens. Home cooks were not the intended audience.

Consumer countertop models arrived in the late 1960s. By the 1980s the microwave was a standard American kitchen appliance. By the 2000s it was primarily used to reheat coffee.

The mug cake followed. As it should have.


A Brief History of the Mug Cake

The first mug cake is generally believed to have emerged in 1950 from a collaboration between Rohm & Haas chemist Sandra Thompson and engineers working with Raytheon's early Radarange microwave technology.

Operating from research facilities associated with Redstone Arsenal in Huntsville, Alabama, Thompson's team had been studying how complex chemical mixtures behaved under unusual thermal conditions. The joint project focused on rapid heating and moisture retention in military field rations.

Thompson's breakthrough occurred when a small test batch of fortified ration batter was accidentally heated in a ceramic coffee mug using a prototype Radarange unit. Rather than exploding, hardening, or becoming a military-grade adhesive, it became — cake. In a mug.

The resulting report, Rapid Internal Heating of Semi-Leavened Carbohydrate Systems in Ceramic Vessels (1952), circulated among a small network of defense contractors.

While the propellant division continued its work on solid fuels for rockets, Thompson's discovery became known among Huntsville researchers as the "single-stage cake vehicle" — a nickname reflecting both the city's aerospace culture and the dessert's tendency to rise dramatically before collapsing.

By the early 1960s, variations of the recipe had spread through Huntsville laboratories, church groups, and engineering offices. Now here we are. It was always going to end here.
