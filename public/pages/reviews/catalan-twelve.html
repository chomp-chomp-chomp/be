The Catalan Twelve — recipes, provenance and the people no longer in the kitchen

Donnie Boman’s curious cookbook turns a fictional culinary conspiracy into a meditation on practical knowledge, inheritance and loss.
The book’s twelve recipes are fully functional; the centuries-spanning culinary society surrounding them is considerably harder to verify.
Arin Bilar
Published 23 September 2026   ·   4 min read

Donnie Boman’s The Catalan Twelve: Twelve Recipes, Recovered is, at once, a cookbook, a fake archival investigation, a comic literary performance, and a memoir about inheritance, loss, and the fragile ways practical knowledge moves from one person to another. The remarkable thing is not just that it attempts all of this, but that it succeeds in making each mode strengthen the others.

The setup is knowingly improbable: a secretive society of twelve bakers has spent centuries moving through kitchens, seeding techniques and recipes, leaving behind only fragmentary ledgers, dubious notes, and arguments over names. The book never asks the reader to believe this literally. In fact, it explicitly loosens that burden almost at once: whether the Twelve exist is separate from whether the recipes work. They do. That distinction turns out to be the book’s governing intelligence. The fictional apparatus is not there to replace the recipes, nor to “elevate” them into literature by disguise. It is there to dramatize what the book is really about: provenance, transmission, attribution, and the difference between having a recipe on paper and having someone beside you in the kitchen.

The codas are where the book keeps its emotional center. Again and again, Boman follows a recipe with a memory that first reads as comic anecdote and then accumulates into a catalogue of absence: a grandmother whose palm was a measuring tool; a mother’s incomplete index card recipe; a cake exploding in an airplane cabin; thrifted pastry tins unused for four years; a father saying “Moonpie moonpie”; a man disappearing into legend after buying a snack cake. None of these moments is inflated. The deadpan remains intact. That tonal restraint is what allows the grief to register without sentimentality. Loss is not introduced late as an emotional twist. It has been present all along, disguised as food memory, family idiom, and regional attachment.

As a cookbook, the book appears to be fully serious and fully usable. The recipe selection is eclectic but coherent at the level of method: xuxos, Southern biscuits, cardamom buns, king cake, sweet potato pie, sticky toffee pudding, gougères, baklava, coca de recapte, pastéis de nata, chocolate roll, and Moon Pies, plus a thirteenth Southern tea cake that pointedly “doesn’t count.” These recipes are not decorative. They carry yields, prep/cook/inactive times, metric measurements, substitution notes, and, crucially, explanations of why things work. A drier choux paste holds more egg; more egg means more steam; steam is the leavener. Baking soda changes pH so dates dissolve into sticky toffee pudding batter. Cream cheese stabilizes a roll filling better than whipped cream alone. Eggs added to hot paste become “scrambled egg with a cheese problem.” That practical clarity matters because the book’s whole argument depends on the recipes being real enough to survive outside the conceit. They are.

The book’s sentiment and structure are not merely well balanced—the structure is what makes the emotional material legible at all. The conspiracy isn’t an eccentric wrapper around a memoir. It is the enabling form of the memoir. A society that can only survive by replacement, by people leaving and arriving, by knowledge passing hand to hand without stable attribution, is already a metaphor for family and cultural inheritance before the book ever says so. When the conclusion turns to the mother’s unfinished recipe card and the line “I’m writing this because there’s nobody standing here. You’re standing there,” the whole apparatus snaps into focus. A written recipe is what you use when there may not be another pair of hands waiting beside you. That is a beautiful and plainspoken truth, and the book earns it.

It carries humor, technical instruction, regional memory, literary gamesmanship, and grief, and it never quite drops any of them. It is a cookbook about baking, a comedy about provenance, and a memoir about the people who are no longer in the kitchen. It understands that names drift, traditions are often invented after the fact, and recipes outlive their authors by belonging to whoever makes them next.

Go make the xuxos again. They’re better the second time.
